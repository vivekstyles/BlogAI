An **economic indicator** is a regularly published number that tells you something about the health of an economy: how fast it is growing, whether people are finding work, how quickly prices are rising, and where things may be heading. Governments, central banks, businesses and investors watch the same handful of indicators, and news headlines usually report them the day they come out.

This guide explains **25 indicators that matter most**, grouped by what they measure. For each one you get what it is, who publishes it, the latest reading where we could verify it, and how to read it without being misled.

**The short answer.** Start with five: **GDP** (growth), the **unemployment rate** and **payrolls** (jobs), **CPI or PCE inflation** (prices), and the central bank's **policy interest rate** (the cost of money). Add the **yield curve**, **PMIs** and **jobless claims** for early warnings, and **oil prices**, **trade** and **government debt** for the global picture. No single number tells the whole story; the signal is in how several of them move together.

**How we wrote this.** Each indicator links to the agency or organization that publishes it. Current readings are taken from official releases and are mostly for the United States, the world's largest economy, plus global figures from the IMF, WTO, EIA and FAO. All were checked as of 1 November 2026. Data are revised often, so check the source for the latest number.

## Three types of indicators

Economists group indicators by **timing**:

- **Leading indicators** tend to move *before* the economy turns. Examples: the yield curve, building permits, new orders in PMIs, jobless claims, stock prices.
- **Coincident indicators** move *with* the economy. Examples: GDP, payrolls, industrial production, retail sales.
- **Lagging indicators** move *after* the economy has already turned. Examples: the unemployment rate (which often keeps rising after a recession ends), inflation, and government debt.

## Growth and output

### 1. Gross domestic product (GDP)

**What it measures.** The total value of all final goods and services produced in a country over a period. It is the broadest measure of economic activity. **Real** GDP removes the effect of inflation, so it shows whether the economy actually produced more.

**Latest reading.** U.S. real GDP grew at an annualized rate of **3.4%** in the third quarter of 2026, according to the advance estimate released on 29 October 2026, led by business investment, consumer spending and government spending ([BEA](https://www.bea.gov/data/gdp/gross-domestic-product)). Globally, the IMF expects growth of **3.1%** in 2026 and 3.4% in 2027 ([IMF, October 2026](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/weo/issues/2026/10/13/world-economic-outlook-october-2026)).

**How to read it.** U.S. quarterly GDP is reported at an *annualized* rate (what growth would be if the quarter's pace lasted a full year). Most other countries report the plain quarter-on-quarter change, so a "0.5%" in Europe is roughly comparable to "2%" in the U.S. Early estimates are often revised by a large amount. A common shorthand defines a recession as two quarters of falling GDP, but in the U.S. the official call is made by a committee at the National Bureau of Economic Research using a wider set of data.

### 2. GDP per capita

**What it measures.** GDP divided by population. It is a rough guide to average living standards and the best single number for comparing countries.

**Where to find it.** The [World Bank](https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/NY.GDP.PCAP.CD) and the IMF publish it for nearly every country. For comparing living standards, use figures adjusted for **purchasing power parity (PPP)**, which account for the fact that the same money buys more in some countries than others.

**How to read it.** GDP per capita is an average, so it says nothing about how income is shared. A country can have high GDP per capita and high inequality at the same time.

### 3. Industrial production

**What it measures.** The output of factories, mines and utilities. In the U.S., the Federal Reserve publishes it monthly in its [G.17 release](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/g17/current/default.htm), along with **capacity utilization**, the share of available capacity in use.

**How to read it.** Industry is a smaller share of rich economies than it used to be, but it swings more than services, so it often shows turning points early. High capacity utilization can signal that firms will need to invest or raise prices.

### 4. Purchasing managers' indexes (PMIs)

**What it measures.** Monthly surveys asking purchasing managers whether orders, output, employment, prices and deliveries are rising or falling. The [Institute for Supply Management](https://www.ismworld.org/supply-management-news-and-reports/reports/ism-report-on-business/) publishes the best-known U.S. indexes; S&P Global publishes PMIs for dozens of countries.

**Latest reading.** The ISM manufacturing PMI was **54.5** in September 2026, its ninth straight month above 50, with new orders at 55.3 (ISM report, 1 October 2026).

**How to read it.** A reading above **50** means more firms report improvement than decline; below 50 means the opposite. PMIs come out at the start of each month, weeks before official data, which makes them one of the earliest signals of a turn. The **new orders** and **prices paid** components are especially useful for spotting future output and inflation.

### 5. Labor productivity

**What it measures.** Output per hour worked. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes it quarterly ([BLS productivity](https://www.bls.gov/productivity/)).

**How to read it.** Productivity is the main driver of long-run living standards, because it lets wages rise without pushing up prices. Quarterly figures are noisy; look at averages over several years. Whether the AI investment boom of 2025 and 2026 will raise productivity growth for long is one of the big open questions in economics today. In October 2026, the Federal Reserve described productivity growth as "strong."

## Jobs and the labor market

### 6. Unemployment rate

**What it measures.** The share of the labor force (people working or actively looking for work) who do not have a job. In the U.S., it comes from a monthly survey of about 60,000 households.

**Latest reading.** **4.2%** in September 2026 ([BLS Employment Situation](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/empsit.nr0.htm), released 2 October 2026).

**How to read it.** The unemployment rate is a lagging indicator: firms are slow to fire at the start of a downturn and slow to hire after it ends. Watch the *direction*. The **Sahm rule**, developed by economist Claudia Sahm, signals the early months of a recession when the three-month average unemployment rate rises 0.5 percentage points above its low of the previous year. The rate does not count people who have given up looking, so read it alongside participation (indicator 11).

### 7. Nonfarm payrolls

**What it measures.** The monthly change in the number of jobs at U.S. employers outside farming, from a survey of businesses. It is released on the first Friday of most months and is one of the most market-moving numbers in the world.

**Latest reading.** **+29,000** jobs in September 2026 ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/empsit.nr0.htm)), a weak figure by historical standards.

**How to read it.** Monthly changes are revised, sometimes by tens of thousands, over the next two months and again each year. The "breakeven" pace needed to keep unemployment steady depends on how fast the labor force is growing, which in turn depends heavily on immigration. Slower population growth means a lower number can still be enough. The Federal Reserve noted in October 2026 that "job gains have kept pace with the workforce."

### 8. Initial jobless claims

**What it measures.** The number of people filing a new claim for unemployment insurance each week. The U.S. Department of Labor publishes it every Thursday ([DOL weekly claims](https://www.dol.gov/ui/data.pdf)).

**How to read it.** Claims are the most up-to-date measure of layoffs. Single weeks are noisy, especially around holidays, so use the four-week average. A steady rise over several weeks has preceded past recessions.

### 9. Job openings (JOLTS)

**What it measures.** The Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey counts open positions, hires, quits and layoffs each month ([BLS JOLTS](https://www.bls.gov/jlt/)).

**How to read it.** The **quits rate** is a good measure of worker confidence: people quit more when they are sure they can find a better job. The ratio of openings to unemployed people shows how tight the labor market is. A falling ratio means employers have more choice and wage growth tends to slow.

### 10. Wage growth

**What it measures.** How fast pay is rising. The quickest measure is average hourly earnings in the monthly jobs report. The quarterly **Employment Cost Index** is more reliable, because it adjusts for changes in the mix of jobs.

**Latest reading.** Average hourly earnings rose **3.0%** over the year to September 2026. After inflation, real hourly earnings fell **0.7%** ([BLS Real Earnings](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/realer.nr0.htm)).

**How to read it.** Compare wage growth with inflation to see whether workers' purchasing power is rising. Central banks also watch wages for signs that inflation is becoming entrenched. We explain what falling real wages mean for a household budget in [10 ways inflation affects your daily life](/articles/how-inflation-affects-daily-life).

### 11. Labor force participation rate

**What it measures.** The share of the working-age population that is either working or looking for work.

**Latest reading.** **61.8%** in the U.S. in September 2026 ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/empsit.nr0.htm)).

**How to read it.** Participation moves slowly and is shaped by long-run forces such as population aging, as more people reach retirement age. For a cleaner read on the job market, economists often watch the participation rate of people aged 25 to 54, which is less affected by retirement and schooling.

## Prices and inflation

### 12. Consumer price index (CPI)

**What it measures.** The change in the price of a fixed basket of goods and services bought by households. In the U.S., the BLS publishes it monthly; most countries have an equivalent.

**Latest reading.** U.S. CPI rose **3.7%** over the 12 months to September 2026. **Core** CPI, which excludes food and energy, rose **2.6%**. Energy rose 20.4% and gasoline 34.2%, after conflict disrupted oil shipments through the Strait of Hormuz ([BLS CPI](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cpi.nr0.htm), released 14 October 2026). The IMF expects global inflation of **4.7%** in 2026.

**How to read it.** The gap between headline and core tells you whether price increases are broad or concentrated. In 2026, the gap points to an energy shock rather than an economy-wide spiral, but central banks worry that high energy prices can spread into wages and other prices if they last.

### 13. Personal consumption expenditures (PCE) price index

**What it measures.** Another measure of consumer prices, published monthly by the Bureau of Economic Analysis. It covers a wider range of spending than the CPI, including what employers and governments pay for health care on households' behalf, and it adjusts faster when people switch between products.

**Latest reading.** **3.6%** over the year to September 2026 ([BEA](https://www.bea.gov/data/personal-consumption-expenditures-price-index), released 29 October 2026).

**How to read it.** The Federal Reserve's **2% inflation target** is defined in terms of PCE, not CPI ([Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/faqs/economy_14400.htm)). PCE inflation usually runs a little below CPI inflation, partly because housing has a smaller weight.

### 14. Producer price index (PPI)

**What it measures.** The prices businesses receive for their goods and services, including raw materials and intermediate goods ([BLS PPI](https://www.bls.gov/ppi/)).

**How to read it.** Changes in producer prices often reach consumers with a lag, so PPI can give an early read on where consumer inflation is going. The link is loose, because firms can absorb cost increases in their profit margins or pass them on.

### 15. Inflation expectations

**What it measures.** What people, businesses and investors expect inflation to be in the future. Sources include the [University of Michigan Surveys of Consumers](https://www.sca.isr.umich.edu/), the New York Fed's Survey of Consumer Expectations, and **breakeven inflation**: the difference between yields on ordinary government bonds and inflation-protected bonds.

**How to read it.** Central banks watch expectations closely, because if people expect high inflation, they demand higher wages and accept higher prices, which can make inflation self-fulfilling. Stable long-term expectations during a temporary price shock are a reassuring sign.

## Money, interest rates and markets

### 16. Central bank policy interest rate

**What it measures.** The short-term interest rate a central bank sets to steer the economy. In the U.S., it is the target range for the **federal funds rate**; in the euro area, the European Central Bank's deposit rate; in the UK, the Bank of England's Bank Rate.

**Latest reading.** The Federal Reserve **raised** its target range by a quarter point to **3.75%–4.00%** in September 2026, after three cuts in late 2025, and held it there on 28 October 2026. Two policymakers voted for another increase ([Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20261028a.htm); [rate history](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/openmarket.htm)).

**How to read it.** Higher rates make borrowing more expensive, slowing spending and inflation; lower rates do the opposite. Policy works with long and variable lags, often a year or more, so central banks act on forecasts, not just today's data. Their statements and projections are indicators in their own right.

### 17. The yield curve

**What it measures.** The difference between interest rates on long-term and short-term government bonds. Normally, long-term rates are higher, because lenders want more to tie up money for longer.

**How to read it.** When short-term rates rise **above** long-term rates, the curve is **inverted**. An inverted curve (often measured as the 10-year Treasury yield minus the 3-month yield) has preceded every U.S. recession since the late 1960s, which is why the New York Fed publishes a recession probability model based on it ([New York Fed](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/capital_markets/ycfaq)). It is not a perfect signal: the curve inverted in 2022 and stayed inverted for a long time without a U.S. recession following, so treat it as a warning, not a forecast.

### 18. Exchange rates

**What it measures.** The price of one currency in terms of another. The Federal Reserve publishes daily exchange rates and a broad trade-weighted index of the dollar ([Fed H.10](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/h10/current/)).

**How to read it.** A stronger currency makes imports cheaper and exports more expensive. Because oil and many other commodities are priced in U.S. dollars, a stronger dollar raises their cost for other countries. Exchange rates respond strongly to differences in interest rates between countries and to shifts in investor risk appetite.

### 19. Stock market indexes

**What it measures.** The combined value of a group of company shares, such as the S&P 500 in the U.S., the FTSE 100 in the UK, or the MSCI World index across developed markets.

**How to read it.** Stock prices reflect investors' expectations of future profits, so they often move before the economy does. They also react to interest rates and sentiment, and they can rise or fall for long periods for reasons unrelated to the wider economy. A few very large companies can dominate an index; in 2025 and 2026, AI-related technology firms drove much of the U.S. market's movement. The economist Paul Samuelson's old joke still applies: the stock market has predicted nine of the last five recessions.

## Consumers and housing

### 20. Retail sales

**What it measures.** Monthly spending at stores, restaurants and online retailers, published by the [U.S. Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/retail/index.html).

**How to read it.** Consumer spending is about two-thirds of U.S. GDP, so retail sales give an early read on growth. The headline figure is **not adjusted for inflation**. In 2026, higher gasoline prices push up sales at gas stations without people buying more fuel, so watch "control group" sales, which exclude autos, gas, building materials and restaurants.

### 21. Consumer sentiment and confidence

**What it measures.** Surveys of how households feel about their finances and the economy. The two main U.S. measures are the [University of Michigan's Index of Consumer Sentiment](https://www.sca.isr.umich.edu/) and the Conference Board's Consumer Confidence Index.

**How to read it.** Sentiment often reacts strongly to gasoline prices, inflation and news, and does not always predict spending; people who say they feel gloomy often keep spending. Large, sustained drops are worth watching, especially when they show up in plans to buy cars, homes or appliances.

### 22. Housing starts and building permits

**What it measures.** The number of new homes on which construction began, and the number of permits issued for future construction, published monthly by the [Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/construction/nrc/index.html).

**How to read it.** Housing is highly sensitive to interest rates, so it often turns first when central banks tighten or ease. Permits lead starts by a few months. With the average 30-year U.S. mortgage rate at **7.24%** in late October 2026 ([FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/MORTGAGE30US)), housing is one of the sectors most exposed to the Fed's rate increase.

## The global picture

### 23. Oil prices

**What it measures.** The price of crude oil, usually quoted as **Brent** (the international benchmark) or **WTI** (the U.S. benchmark), in dollars per barrel.

**Latest reading.** The U.S. Energy Information Administration expects Brent to average **\$96 a barrel** in 2026 and \$105 in the fourth quarter, assuming Middle East oil flows stay constrained through the end of the year, before falling to an average of \$84 in 2027 ([EIA Short-Term Energy Outlook](https://www.eia.gov/outlooks/steo/), 6 October 2026).

**How to read it.** Oil feeds into transport, plastics, fertilizer and heating, so it affects inflation and growth worldwide. Sharp oil price spikes have come before several global slowdowns. The effect depends on whether a country imports or exports energy: the IMF's October 2026 outlook says the toughest prospects are for energy-importing economies outside the technology supply chain.

### 24. World trade volume

**What it measures.** The growth in the physical volume of goods and services traded between countries, tracked by the World Trade Organization.

**Latest reading.** The WTO raised its forecast for growth in world merchandise trade volume in 2026 to **3.9%**, from 1.9% in March, and projects 4.1% in 2027. It said AI-related goods such as semiconductors and servers accounted for **47%** of the growth in goods trade in the first half of 2026, while lower shipments of oil, gas and fertilizer weighed on trade. It cut its forecast for services trade, hit by disrupted travel in the Middle East ([WTO, 8 October 2026](https://www.wto.org/english/news_e/news26_e/stat_08oct26_510_e.htm)).

**How to read it.** Trade is more volatile than GDP and very sensitive to tariffs, conflicts and shipping costs. The **trade balance** (exports minus imports) for a single country is often misread: a deficit is not in itself a sign of weakness, and it reflects saving and investment patterns as much as trade policy.

### 25. Government debt and deficits

**What it measures.** How much a government owes (debt) and how much it borrows each year (deficit), both usually shown as a percentage of GDP.

**Latest reading.** The IMF estimates global public debt at just under **94% of world GDP** in 2025 and projects it will reach **100% by 2029** ([IMF Fiscal Monitor, April 2026](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/FM/Issues/2026/04/15/fiscal-monitor-april-2026)). Its October 2026 outlook calls for governments to "rebuild fiscal space" urgently.

**How to read it.** There is no single safe level of debt; it depends on interest rates, growth, and whether the debt is in a country's own currency. The key comparison is between the interest rate on the debt and the economy's growth rate: when interest rates exceed growth, debt becomes harder to stabilize without tighter budgets.

## Composite indicators worth knowing

Some organizations combine many indicators into a single index:

- **The Conference Board Leading Economic Index (LEI)** combines ten U.S. indicators, including jobless claims, new orders, building permits, stock prices, the interest rate spread and consumer expectations, into one forward-looking measure ([Conference Board](https://www.conference-board.org/topics/us-leading-indicators)).
- **The New York Fed's Global Supply Chain Pressure Index** combines shipping costs and PMI delivery times to show how stretched global supply chains are ([New York Fed](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/policy/gscpi)).
- **The FAO Food Price Index** tracks international prices of cereals, vegetable oils, dairy, meat and sugar. It averaged **136.0** points in September 2026, up **5.8%** from a year earlier and 15% below its March 2022 peak ([FAO](https://www.fao.org/worldfoodsituation/foodpricesindex/en/)).

## How to read economic data without being misled

1. **Look at trends, not single releases.** Monthly data are noisy and revised. Three-month averages are more reliable.
2. **Check what is being compared.** Month-on-month, quarter-on-quarter annualized and year-on-year changes can tell very different stories.
3. **Adjust for inflation.** Spending, wages and sales figures look stronger in nominal terms when prices are rising fast.
4. **Compare with expectations.** Markets react to the gap between the figure and what forecasters expected, not to the figure alone.
5. **Read indicators together.** In 2026, strong GDP growth alongside weak hiring, high energy prices and a central bank raising rates points to an economy pulled in different directions. That is a more useful picture than any one number gives.

## FAQ

### What are the most important economic indicators?

For a quick read on any economy, watch real GDP growth, the unemployment rate, inflation (CPI or PCE), and the central bank's policy rate. Add PMIs and jobless claims for early warning signs.

### Which indicators predict a recession?

No indicator predicts recessions reliably on its own. The most watched warning signs are an inverted yield curve, rising jobless claims, PMI new orders below 50, falling building permits, and the Sahm rule based on unemployment. A recession is more likely when several of these point the same way.

### Where can I find economic data for free?

National statistics offices and central banks publish their data free of charge. For the U.S., the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis's FRED database collects hundreds of thousands of series in one place. For global comparisons, use the IMF World Economic Outlook database, the World Bank's data site and the OECD.
