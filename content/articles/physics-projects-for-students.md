A good physics project starts with a question you can test, not with a model to build. "How does a pendulum work?" is a topic. "How does the length of a pendulum affect its period?" is a project: you can change one thing, measure another, and compare your results with theory.

This list gives you 101 physics project ideas in three levels. Each project has a **question** to answer, a **method** to follow, the **skills** it builds, and a way to **go further** that turns a class exercise into something worth presenting at a science fair.

**How to use this list.** Projects 1–35 suit beginners and younger students: household materials, simple measurements and clear results. Projects 36–70 add quantitative analysis, graphs, uncertainty and phone or video sensors. Projects 71–101 need school-lab equipment, electronics, programming or public scientific data, and suit senior high school and early university students. You do not have to stay in your level. A beginner project done carefully, with repeated trials and an honest uncertainty estimate, is better than an advanced one done badly.

Many beginner projects grow out of quick demonstrations in our [101 physics experiments you can do at home](/articles/physics-experiments-at-home). We have not run each project for this article; methods follow standard school-lab practice described in the OpenStax textbooks and the tools linked below, and your results will depend on your equipment.

## How to plan a physics project

Every project below follows the same structure. Plan these steps before you start:

1. **Write a testable question.** Name what you will change and what you will measure: "How does X affect Y?"
2. **Identify your variables.** The *independent variable* is what you change. The *dependent variable* is what you measure. *Control variables* are everything you keep the same.
3. **Make a prediction from physics.** Use a formula or concept to predict the shape of the result, for example "period should be proportional to the square root of length". Our [100 physics formulas every student should know](/articles/physics-formulas-every-student-should-know) is a good starting point.
4. **Plan your measurements.** Choose at least five values of the independent variable, and repeat each measurement at least three times.
5. **Estimate uncertainty.** Note the precision of your instruments and the spread of your repeated readings. Report results as a value plus or minus an uncertainty.
6. **Graph your data.** Plot the dependent variable against the independent variable. If theory predicts a straight line when you plot something else (such as $T^2$ against $L$), plot that and find the gradient.
7. **Compare with theory and explain differences.** Disagreement is not failure. Explaining *why* your result differs, such as air resistance, friction or heat loss, is often the best part of a project.

### Useful free tools

- **[phyphox](https://phyphox.org/)**, a free app from RWTH Aachen University, reads your phone's accelerometer, gyroscope, magnetometer, light sensor, barometer and microphone, and exports the data.
- **[Tracker](https://physlets.org/tracker/)**, free open-source video analysis software by Douglas Brown, tracks objects frame by frame in a video and plots position, velocity and acceleration.
- **A spreadsheet or Python** for graphs, trend lines and averages.
- **An Arduino or similar microcontroller** for automated sensor readings in advanced projects.

### Safety and science fair rules

Check your school's rules and any competition rules before you start. The Regeneron International Science and Engineering Fair (ISEF) requires a risk assessment before experimentation for projects involving hazardous chemicals, activities or devices, and some projects need a qualified supervisor ([Society for Science](https://www.societyforscience.org/isef/international-rules/hazardous-chemicals-activities-or-devices/)). The rules are updated every year, so use the current edition. In general:

- Get adult or teacher supervision for heat, high voltage, lasers, strong magnets, pressurised containers, cryogenic materials and radiation sources.
- Never work with mains electricity unless a qualified adult designs and supervises the setup.
- Use only radioactive sources supplied and supervised by a school, or natural background radiation.
- Record your safety precautions in your project log; judges look for them.

## Beginner projects (1–35)

These need household materials and a ruler, timer, kitchen scale or thermometer.

### 1. Bounce height and drop height

**Question:** How does the height a ball is dropped from affect how high it bounces?

**Method:** Drop a ball from five heights between 0.5 m and 2.0 m in front of a tape measure, filming in slow motion to read the bounce height. Repeat three times at each height.

**Skills:** Controlled variables, graphing, energy loss.

**Go further:** Calculate the ratio of bounce height to drop height for different balls and surfaces. That ratio tells you what fraction of the energy survives each bounce.

### 2. Friction on different surfaces

**Question:** Which floor surface produces the most friction on a shoe?

**Method:** Pull a shoe with a spring scale or a rubber band and ruler across carpet, tile, wood and a rubber mat. Record the force needed to keep it moving steadily.

**Skills:** Measuring force, fair testing.

**Go further:** Add weights inside the shoe. Plot friction against weight; the gradient is the coefficient of friction for that surface.

### 3. Paper aeroplane wing shape

**Question:** How does wing shape affect how far a paper aeroplane flies?

**Method:** Fold three designs from the same paper. Launch each 10 times from the same height with the same throw, ideally using a simple rubber-band launcher for consistency. Measure distances.

**Skills:** Reducing variation, averages, aerodynamics.

**Go further:** Change one feature at a time, such as wing area or a paperclip on the nose, to separate their effects.

### 4. What affects a pendulum's period?

**Question:** Does the period of a pendulum depend on its length, its mass or the angle it starts from?

**Method:** Time 10 swings for at least five lengths, then for three masses at fixed length, then for several starting angles.

**Skills:** Testing one variable at a time, timing technique.

**Go further:** Plot period against the square root of length. A straight line confirms $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$. Notice the period starts to increase slightly at large angles.

### 5. Parachute size and fall time

**Question:** How does the area of a parachute affect how long it takes to fall?

**Method:** Make square parachutes from bin bags with sides from 20 cm to 60 cm, all carrying the same weight. Drop each from the same height and time the fall.

**Skills:** Drag, area calculations, repeat measurements.

**Go further:** Test whether fall time depends on area or on side length, by plotting both.

### 6. Ramp angle and rolling distance

**Question:** How does the angle of a ramp affect how far a toy car rolls after leaving it?

**Method:** Release the car from the same point on a ramp set to five angles. Measure the distance it travels across the floor.

**Skills:** Energy conversion, measuring angles.

**Go further:** Keep the starting *height* fixed and change only the angle. Does the car still go further on a steeper ramp?

### 7. Does mass affect how far a toy car rolls?

**Question:** If you add mass to a toy car, does it roll further after a ramp?

**Method:** Tape coins to the car to change its mass, release from a fixed height and measure distance.

**Skills:** Challenging intuition, friction and inertia.

**Go further:** Explain your result using energy and the forces that slow the car, such as rolling resistance and air resistance.

### 8. Balloon rocket distance

**Question:** How does the amount of air in a balloon affect how far a balloon rocket travels along a string?

**Method:** Measure balloon circumference to control how much it is inflated. Release along a string track and measure distance.

**Skills:** Newton's third law, controlling a variable indirectly.

**Go further:** Compare a level string with one sloping upwards.

### 9. Rubber-band launcher

**Question:** How does stretching a rubber band further affect how far it launches a small object?

**Method:** Mount a rubber band between two nails on a board. Pull it back by measured distances and launch a cotton bud or pom-pom horizontally. Measure the range. Wear safety glasses and never aim at people.

**Skills:** Elastic potential energy, projectile motion.

**Go further:** Elastic energy is roughly proportional to the stretch squared. Is the range proportional to the stretch, or to its square?

### 10. Egg-drop protection

**Question:** Which packaging design lets an egg survive the greatest drop?

**Method:** Build containers from fixed materials (paper, straws, tape). Drop each from increasing heights until the egg breaks.

**Skills:** Impulse, engineering design.

**Go further:** Film the impacts in slow motion and estimate the stopping time. Longer stopping times mean smaller forces.

### 11. Best insulator for a hot drink

**Question:** Which material keeps a cup of hot water warm for longest?

**Method:** Wrap identical cups in foil, wool, bubble wrap and paper, and leave one bare. Fill each with water at the same starting temperature and record the temperature every 5 minutes for an hour. *Adult* to pour the hot water.

**Skills:** Cooling curves, heat transfer.

**Go further:** Add a lid to one cup to see how much heat escapes by evaporation and convection from the top.

### 12. Colour and solar heating

**Question:** Does the colour of a container affect how quickly water inside it warms in sunlight?

**Method:** Paint or wrap identical jars in black, white, red and silver. Put the same amount of water in each and record temperatures in the sun every 10 minutes.

**Skills:** Radiation, absorption and reflection.

**Go further:** Repeat on a cloudy day or under a lamp to compare light sources.

### 13. What melts ice fastest?

**Question:** Do salt, sugar and sand melt ice at different rates?

**Method:** Put equal ice cubes on plates and sprinkle equal masses of each substance on them. Measure the meltwater mass or volume every 10 minutes.

**Skills:** Freezing point depression, measurement of rates.

**Go further:** Explain why roads are salted rather than sugared, and why salt stops working in very cold weather.

### 14. Does salt change boiling point?

**Question:** How much does adding salt raise the boiling point of water?

**Method:** With an *adult*, measure the boiling temperature of pure water, then of water with 1, 2 and 3 tablespoons of salt per litre, using a digital thermometer.

**Skills:** Precise thermometry, small effects.

**Go further:** Compare your measured increase with what you would expect from a chemistry textbook. You will find the effect is small, which tests your measurement precision.

### 15. Which fruits and vegetables float?

**Question:** Can you predict whether a fruit floats from its density?

**Method:** Measure each item's mass on a kitchen scale and its volume by displacement in a measuring jug. Calculate density, then test whether it floats.

**Skills:** Density, displacement method.

**Go further:** Explain items that float even though they look dense, such as an orange with its peel, compared with the same orange peeled.

### 16. Clay boat design

**Question:** Which boat shape carries the most load before sinking?

**Method:** Use equal amounts of modelling clay to make several hull shapes. Add coins one at a time until each sinks.

**Skills:** Buoyancy, design iteration.

**Go further:** Measure each boat's volume and compare its maximum load with Archimedes' principle.

### 17. Soap and surface tension

**Question:** How does the amount of soap affect how many water drops fit on a coin?

**Method:** Make water with 0, 1, 2, 5 and 10 drops of washing-up liquid per cup. Count drops added to a coin before spilling. Repeat five times each.

**Skills:** Surface tension, dilution series.

**Go further:** Try salty water, sugary water and water at different temperatures.

### 18. Capillary action in paper

**Question:** Which type of paper draws water up highest in 10 minutes?

**Method:** Hang strips of paper towel, printer paper, newspaper and filter paper with their ends in coloured water. Measure the height reached.

**Skills:** Capillarity, timing.

**Go further:** Look at the papers under a magnifier and relate fibre structure to the result.

### 19. Water level and pitch in bottles

**Question:** How does the water level in a bottle change the note when you tap it and when you blow across it?

**Method:** Fill identical bottles to different levels. Use a free tuner or spectrum app to record the frequency for tapping and blowing.

**Skills:** Sound, resonance, measuring frequency.

**Go further:** For blowing, plot frequency against the length of the air column above the water.

### 20. String telephone

**Question:** How do string type and length affect how well a string telephone carries sound?

**Method:** Make phones with cotton, nylon fishing line and wire, at several lengths. Play a steady tone into one cup and measure loudness at the other with a phone decibel app.

**Skills:** Sound transmission, fair testing.

**Go further:** Test whether pulling the string tighter improves the sound.

### 21. Soundproofing materials

**Question:** Which material blocks sound best?

**Method:** Put a phone playing a fixed tone inside a box, line it with different materials (foam, egg boxes, towels, cardboard) and measure the sound level outside with a second phone.

**Skills:** Absorption and reflection of sound, decibel scale.

**Go further:** Test low and high frequencies separately; many materials block high notes better than low ones.

### 22. Rubber-band guitar

**Question:** How do tension and length affect the pitch of a plucked rubber band?

**Method:** Stretch bands over an open box. Change tension by hanging weights from one end, and change length with a moveable bridge. Measure frequency with a tuner app.

**Skills:** Standing waves, pitch.

**Go further:** Compare with $f = \frac{1}{2L}\sqrt{T/\mu}$: does frequency go up with the square root of tension?

### 23. Pinhole size and image sharpness

**Question:** How does pinhole diameter affect the sharpness and brightness of a pinhole camera image?

**Method:** Make holes of different sizes in foil. Photograph the image of a bright object on the screen and compare.

**Skills:** Straight-line propagation of light, trade-offs.

**Go further:** Very small holes blur the image again because of diffraction. Find the hole size that gives the sharpest image.

### 24. Shadow size and distance

**Question:** How does the distance between a light and an object change the size of its shadow?

**Method:** Shine a small torch at a cardboard shape in front of a wall. Measure the shadow size for several torch distances.

**Skills:** Similar triangles, measurement.

**Go further:** Use a large light source and measure the blurry edge (penumbra) as well.

### 25. Mirrors and multiple images

**Question:** How does the angle between two mirrors affect the number of images you see?

**Method:** Hinge two mirrors with tape, set them at measured angles and count images of a small object.

**Skills:** Reflection, finding a rule from data.

**Go further:** Test whether the number of images follows $360°/\theta - 1$, and what happens at angles that do not divide 360° evenly.

### 26. Which liquid bends light most?

**Question:** Do water, cooking oil, salt water and sugar syrup bend light by different amounts?

**Method:** Put a coin under a glass of each liquid. Measure the real depth and the apparent depth by looking from directly above with a ruler alongside.

**Skills:** Refraction, refractive index.

**Go further:** Refractive index is approximately real depth ÷ apparent depth. Compare your values with published ones.

### 27. Fruit and vegetable batteries

**Question:** Which fruit or vegetable produces the highest voltage?

**Method:** Insert a zinc-coated nail and a copper strip into lemons, potatoes, apples and oranges at a fixed distance apart. Measure the voltage with a multimeter.

**Skills:** Electrochemical cells, using a multimeter.

**Go further:** Keep the fruit the same and change the metals or the distance between electrodes.

### 28. Electromagnet strength

**Question:** How does the number of turns of wire affect the strength of an electromagnet?

**Method:** Wind 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50 turns around a nail and count the paperclips it lifts from a single AA battery. Connect only briefly so the wire does not overheat.

**Skills:** Magnetism, fair testing.

**Go further:** Compare iron nails, steel bolts and a plastic pen as the core.

### 29. The triboelectric series

**Question:** Which pairs of materials produce the strongest static charge when rubbed?

**Method:** Rub balloons with wool, cotton, plastic and hair for a fixed number of strokes. Measure how far the balloon can lift tiny pieces of paper, or how much it deflects a stream of water.

**Skills:** Electric charge, designing a measurement.

**Go further:** Repeat on a humid day and a dry day.

### 30. Magnets and temperature

**Question:** Does temperature affect a magnet's strength?

**Method:** Cool a ceramic fridge magnet in the freezer, warm one in hot tap water (sealed in a bag) and keep one at room temperature. Count paperclips each lifts, or the number of cards it holds a clip through.

**Skills:** Magnetism, temperature control.

**Go further:** Research the Curie temperature, above which a magnet loses its magnetism. Do not heat neodymium magnets: they can be permanently weakened and may crack.

### 31. Oersted's discovery

**Question:** How does the current in a wire affect how far a nearby compass needle turns?

**Method:** Run a wire over a compass, aligned north-south. Connect one, then two AA batteries in series with a small resistor (about 10 Ω) for a moment and measure the deflection.

**Skills:** Magnetic effect of a current, electrical safety.

**Go further:** Change the distance between wire and compass. The field from a wire falls off as $1/r$.

### 32. Solar oven design

**Question:** Which solar oven design reaches the highest temperature?

**Method:** Build pizza-box ovens with different reflector angles, lining colours and window materials. Measure temperature inside every 10 minutes in full sun.

**Skills:** Radiation, insulation, design.

**Go further:** Track the Sun by adjusting the oven every 15 minutes and compare with a fixed oven.

### 33. Paper helicopter blades

**Question:** How does blade length affect the fall time of a paper helicopter?

**Method:** Make helicopters with blades from 4 cm to 12 cm long, using the same paper and one paperclip each. Drop from the same height and time the fall.

**Skills:** Drag, rotation.

**Go further:** Find the blade length that gives the longest fall time, and explain why longer is not always better.

### 34. Spinning top design

**Question:** How does the shape and mass distribution of a top affect how long it spins?

**Method:** Make tops from card discs and pencils, varying disc diameter or adding coins at the edge versus the centre. Spin with the same twist and time until they fall.

**Skills:** Moment of inertia, angular momentum.

**Go further:** Film from above and count rotations per second in slow motion.

### 35. Build and calibrate a sundial

**Question:** How accurately can a homemade sundial tell the time?

**Method:** Set a stick vertically in flat ground or on a board. Mark the shadow's position every hour on a sunny day, then compare readings with a clock over the following weeks.

**Skills:** Earth's rotation, angles, long-term observation.

**Go further:** Notice how readings drift over weeks; research the "equation of time".

## Intermediate projects (36–70)

These need graphs, uncertainties and often a phone sensor app, video analysis or a multimeter.

### 36. Measure g with a pendulum

**Question:** How precisely can you measure the acceleration due to gravity with a simple pendulum?

**Method:** Time 20 swings for at least six lengths from 0.3 m to 1.5 m. Plot $T^2$ against $L$; the gradient equals $4\pi^2/g$.

**Skills:** Linearising data, uncertainty from a gradient.

**Go further:** Calculate the percentage uncertainty in $g$ and find which measurement contributes most to it.

### 37. Projectile motion with video analysis

**Question:** Does a thrown ball follow the parabola predicted by kinematics?

**Method:** Film a ball thrown across a plain wall with a metre stick in the frame. Track it in Tracker and fit the vertical position to a quadratic.

**Skills:** Video analysis, curve fitting.

**Go further:** Compare a heavy ball with a light foam ball. Where does air resistance become visible in the data?

### 38. Terminal velocity of coffee filters

**Question:** How does the mass of a falling object affect its terminal velocity?

**Method:** Stack 1 to 8 paper coffee filters and drop each stack. Use video or a motion sensor to find the steady speed.

**Skills:** Drag models, graph analysis.

**Go further:** Plot terminal velocity squared against mass. A straight line suggests drag is proportional to $v^2$.

### 39. Ball bounce and temperature

**Question:** How does temperature affect how well a ball bounces?

**Method:** Bring squash balls or tennis balls to different temperatures in a fridge, at room temperature and in warm water (dried before use). Measure drop and bounce heights quickly.

**Skills:** Coefficient of restitution, controlling temperature.

**Go further:** Research why squash players warm the ball up before a game.

### 40. Damped oscillations

**Question:** How does the liquid a mass oscillates in affect how quickly the oscillation dies away?

**Method:** Hang a mass on a spring with its lower half in air, water, then oil. Film it and measure the amplitude of successive swings.

**Skills:** Damping, exponential decay.

**Go further:** Plot the logarithm of amplitude against time to find a damping constant for each liquid.

### 41. Rolling race of shapes

**Question:** Can you predict which shape rolls down a ramp fastest?

**Method:** Race a solid cylinder, hollow cylinder, solid ball and hollow ball of similar size. Time each over the same ramp.

**Skills:** Moment of inertia, energy conservation.

**Go further:** Calculate predicted times from $I$ for each shape and compare with your measurements.

### 42. Conservation of momentum in collisions

**Question:** Is momentum conserved when two carts collide?

**Method:** Use toy carts or skateboards on a smooth floor, with Velcro for sticky collisions. Film the collisions and measure velocities before and after.

**Skills:** Momentum, measuring velocity from video.

**Go further:** Calculate kinetic energy before and after to classify each collision as elastic or inelastic.

### 43. Rubber band elasticity and hysteresis

**Question:** Does a rubber band obey Hooke's law, and does it behave the same when unloaded?

**Method:** Add weights in steps and measure the length, then remove them in steps and measure again.

**Skills:** Force-extension graphs, non-linear materials.

**Go further:** The area between the loading and unloading curves is energy lost as heat. Estimate it.

### 44. Bicycle gears

**Question:** How does gear ratio affect the speed of a bicycle at a fixed pedalling rate?

**Method:** Count the teeth on the front chainrings and rear sprockets. Measure the distance travelled per pedal turn for several gears.

**Skills:** Ratios, rotational motion, real-world measurement.

**Go further:** Use a phone's GPS or a cycle computer to measure speed at a steady cadence and compare with your predictions.

### 45. Newton's law of cooling

**Question:** Does a cup of hot water cool exponentially towards room temperature?

**Method:** Log the temperature every minute for an hour with a digital thermometer. Plot the logarithm of the temperature difference against time.

**Skills:** Exponential models, linearisation.

**Go further:** Compare cooling constants for different cup materials, or with and without a lid.

### 46. Specific heat capacity of metals

**Question:** What is the specific heat capacity of aluminium, steel or copper?

**Method:** Heat a known mass of metal in boiling water (*adult*), then transfer it to a known mass of cold water in an insulated cup. Measure the final temperature and use $mc\Delta T$ to balance heat lost and gained.

**Skills:** Method of mixtures, heat loss.

**Go further:** Estimate how much heat is lost to the cup and air, and correct for it.

### 47. Latent heat of fusion of ice

**Question:** How much energy does it take to melt a kilogram of ice?

**Method:** Add a weighed amount of melting ice (at 0 °C, patted dry with a paper towel) to a known mass of warm water in an insulated cup. Measure temperatures before and after melting.

**Skills:** Energy balance, latent heat.

**Go further:** Compare your result with the accepted value of about 334 kJ/kg and identify the main source of error.

### 48. Thermal conductivity comparison

**Question:** Which of several rods conducts heat fastest?

**Method:** Stand rods of copper, aluminium, steel and glass of the same size in hot water. Measure the temperature at the top with a thermometer or a thermal camera over time.

**Skills:** Conduction, rates of change.

**Go further:** Rank the rods and compare with published thermal conductivity values.

### 49. Insulation in a model house

**Question:** How does insulation thickness affect heat loss from a model house?

**Method:** Build cardboard boxes with different thicknesses of insulation. Heat each with the same warm water bottle and log the internal temperature drop.

**Skills:** Heat transfer, engineering trade-offs.

**Go further:** Add a "window" of cling film to see how much heat it loses compared with an insulated wall.

### 50. Speed of sound with a resonance tube

**Question:** What is the speed of sound in air at room temperature?

**Method:** Hold a tube vertically in a tall jug of water and play a tone of known frequency from a phone above it. Raise the tube until the sound is loudest. That air column is a quarter wavelength (plus a small end correction).

**Skills:** Resonance, standing waves.

**Go further:** Use two resonance lengths to cancel the end correction. Measure on a cold and a warm day and compare with $v \approx 331 + 0.6\,T_C$.

### 51. Measure the Doppler effect

**Question:** Can you measure a moving source's speed from the Doppler shift?

**Method:** Play a steady tone from a phone attached to a bicycle or carried by a person riding past a second phone running a frequency analyser. Measure the frequency approaching and leaving.

**Skills:** Doppler equation, data analysis.

**Go further:** Calculate the speed from the frequency shift and compare with the speed measured by timing over a known distance.

### 52. Standing waves on a string

**Question:** How does tension change the frequencies of standing waves on a string?

**Method:** Drive a string with a small speaker or vibration motor connected to a phone tone generator. Hang weights to set the tension and find the frequencies that produce 1, 2 and 3 loops.

**Skills:** Harmonics, wave speed.

**Go further:** Plot frequency against the square root of tension to test $v = \sqrt{T/\mu}$.

### 53. Chladni plate patterns

**Question:** How do vibration patterns on a plate change with frequency?

**Method:** Fix a thin metal plate at its centre on a speaker. Sprinkle salt on it and sweep the frequency with a tone generator app, photographing each pattern.

**Skills:** Resonance in two dimensions, observation.

**Go further:** Compare square and circular plates, or plates of different thicknesses.

### 54. Bottle resonance

**Question:** How does the volume of air in a bottle affect the note it makes when you blow across it?

**Method:** Add water to change the air volume and measure the frequency with a phone app.

**Skills:** Helmholtz resonance, fitting a model.

**Go further:** Test whether frequency is proportional to $1/\sqrt{V}$, as the Helmholtz resonator model predicts.

### 55. Measure refractive index with a laser

**Question:** What is the refractive index of a glass or acrylic block?

**Method:** Shine a low-power laser (Class 2, *adult* supervision) into a rectangular block at several angles on a sheet of paper. Trace the rays and measure angles with a protractor.

**Skills:** Snell's law, graph of $\sin\theta_1$ against $\sin\theta_2$.

**Go further:** Measure the critical angle with a semicircular block and compare.

### 56. Testing the lens equation

**Question:** Does a magnifying glass obey the thin lens equation?

**Method:** Form images of a bright object on a screen at different object distances. Measure object and image distances.

**Skills:** Optics, reciprocal graphs.

**Go further:** Plot $1/d_i$ against $1/d_o$. The intercepts should both equal $1/f$.

### 57. Track spacing on a CD or DVD

**Question:** How far apart are the data tracks on a CD and a DVD?

**Method:** Shine a laser of known wavelength through or off the disc (*adult* supervision) onto a wall and measure the angle of the diffraction spots. Use $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$.

**Skills:** Diffraction gratings, trigonometry.

**Go further:** Compare your values with the published track pitch of each format.

### 58. Width of a human hair by diffraction

**Question:** Can you measure the width of a hair with a laser?

**Method:** Tape a hair across a frame and shine a laser at it, projecting the pattern on a wall several metres away (*adult* supervision). Measure the spacing of the dark fringes.

**Skills:** Single-slit diffraction (by Babinet's principle, a hair diffracts like a slit of the same width).

**Go further:** Compare hairs from several people and check against a microscope measurement if available.

### 59. Malus's law

**Question:** How does the intensity of light through two polarisers depend on the angle between them?

**Method:** Shine a light through two polarising filters onto a phone's light sensor in phyphox. Rotate one filter in 10° steps.

**Skills:** Polarisation, cosine relationships.

**Go further:** Plot intensity against $\cos^2\theta$ to test Malus's law.

### 60. Ohmic and non-ohmic components

**Question:** Does a filament bulb obey Ohm's law?

**Method:** Using a low-voltage supply or batteries, measure current through a resistor and a small filament bulb at several voltages.

**Skills:** I-V curves, using two meters.

**Go further:** Explain the bulb's curve by relating its resistance to filament temperature.

### 61. Internal resistance of a battery

**Question:** How much internal resistance does an AA battery have, and does it change as the battery runs down?

**Method:** Connect resistors of several values across the battery, measuring terminal voltage and current each time. Plot voltage against current.

**Skills:** EMF and internal resistance, straight-line graphs.

**Go further:** Compare new and used batteries, and different brands or chemistries.

### 62. Resistance of pencil lines

**Question:** How does the length and darkness of a pencil line affect its resistance?

**Method:** Draw graphite strips with pencils from 2H to 6B. Measure resistance with a multimeter at several lengths.

**Skills:** Resistivity, sample preparation.

**Go further:** Draw lines of different widths and test whether resistance follows $R = \rho L/A$.

### 63. Capacitor charging curve

**Question:** Does a capacitor charge exponentially with a time constant of $RC$?

**Method:** Charge a large capacitor (such as 1,000 µF) through a resistor from a battery, reading the voltage every few seconds with a multimeter or an Arduino.

**Skills:** Exponential growth, time constants.

**Go further:** Repeat with different resistors and plot time constant against $R$.

### 64. Motor speed and voltage

**Question:** How does supply voltage affect the speed of a small DC motor?

**Method:** Run a small hobby motor from one to four AA batteries. Measure its speed using a phone's slow-motion video with a marked paper flag, or a phone's sound spectrum.

**Skills:** Motors, measuring rotation rate.

**Go further:** Add a load, such as winding up a string with a weight, and measure the power output.

### 65. Induced voltage and magnet speed

**Question:** How does the speed of a magnet through a coil affect the induced voltage?

**Method:** Drop a magnet through a coil from different heights and record the voltage with a data logger, a microcontroller or a multimeter with a peak hold.

**Skills:** Faraday's law, data logging.

**Go further:** Change the number of turns and compare with $\varepsilon = -N\,\Delta\Phi/\Delta t$.

### 66. Eddy current braking

**Question:** How does the metal and thickness of a tube affect how slowly a magnet falls through it?

**Method:** Time a neodymium magnet falling through copper and aluminium tubes of different wall thicknesses (*adult* for magnets).

**Skills:** Lenz's law, electrical conductivity.

**Go further:** Compare a slotted tube with an unslotted one to show that eddy currents cause the effect.

### 67. Solar panel angle

**Question:** How does the tilt angle of a small solar panel affect its power output?

**Method:** Connect a small panel to a resistor and multimeter. Measure power at tilt angles from 0° to 90° at midday.

**Skills:** Electrical power, angle of incidence.

**Go further:** Repeat at different times of day and compare the best angle with your latitude.

### 68. Wind turbine blades

**Question:** How do the number and angle of turbine blades affect power output?

**Method:** Build a turbine from a small DC motor (as generator) and card blades. Use a household fan at a fixed distance and measure output voltage across a resistor.

**Skills:** Energy conversion, engineering optimisation.

**Go further:** Measure the wind speed with a phone anemometer attachment or by timing a ribbon, and estimate the turbine's efficiency.

### 69. Measure Earth's magnetic field

**Question:** What is the strength and direction of Earth's magnetic field where you live?

**Method:** Use phyphox's magnetometer, away from metal objects, to read the field components. Compare with a model value from [NOAA's magnetic field calculator](https://www.ngdc.noaa.gov/geomag/calculators/magcalc.shtml).

**Skills:** Vector components, sensor calibration.

**Go further:** Build a tangent galvanometer: a coil with a compass at its centre, where the needle's deflection depends on the ratio of the coil's field to Earth's.

### 70. Inverse-square law for light

**Question:** Does the intensity of light fall off with the square of the distance?

**Method:** In a dark room, measure the illuminance from a small bulb with a phone's light sensor at distances from 0.2 m to 2 m.

**Skills:** Inverse-square relationships, log-log graphs.

**Go further:** Plot log intensity against log distance. A gradient of −2 confirms the law; explain deviations close to the bulb.

## Advanced projects (71–101)

These need school-lab equipment, electronics, programming, public scientific data or a qualified supervisor.

### 71. Planck's constant from LEDs

**Question:** Can you measure Planck's constant using LEDs of different colours?

**Method:** Find the voltage at which each LED just begins to conduct and glow. Plot that threshold voltage against $1/\lambda$ using each LED's stated wavelength. The gradient equals $hc/e$.

**Skills:** Semiconductor physics, systematic uncertainty.

**Go further:** Discuss why defining the "turn-on" point is the largest source of error, and try different methods of finding it from an I-V curve.

### 72. Speed of light by time of flight

**Question:** Can you measure the speed of light directly in a school lab?

**Method:** Send fast light pulses from an LED or laser over a long path, or through a long optical fibre, to a fast photodiode. Measure the delay on an oscilloscope as the path length changes.

**Skills:** High-speed electronics, timing.

**Go further:** Measure the speed of light in the fibre and calculate the fibre's refractive index.

### 73. Cavendish experiment

**Question:** Can you measure the gravitational constant $G$ with a homemade torsion balance?

**Method:** Hang a light rod with small lead or steel masses from a thin fibre inside a draught-proof box. Bring large masses close and track the rotation with a laser spot on a distant wall.

**Skills:** Very small forces, torsion pendulums, long data runs.

**Go further:** Compare your value with the CODATA value of $6.674 \times 10^{-11}$ N·m²/kg² and identify the main sources of error. Commercial school torsion balances also exist.

### 74. Eratosthenes: measure Earth's circumference

**Question:** Can you measure Earth's size with a stick and a shadow?

**Method:** Partner with a school several hundred kilometres north or south of you. Both measure the length of a vertical stick's shadow at local solar noon on the same day. The difference in Sun angles and the distance between schools give Earth's circumference.

**Skills:** Geometry, collaboration, historical science.

**Go further:** Collect data from several partner schools to reduce uncertainty.

### 75. Cloud chamber

**Question:** What kinds of particle tracks can you see from background radiation?

**Method:** Build a diffusion cloud chamber from a clear container, felt soaked in isopropyl alcohol and a metal base cooled with dry ice. *Qualified adult supervision only:* isopropyl alcohol is flammable and dry ice causes cold burns.

**Skills:** Particle physics, observation, safety planning.

**Go further:** Classify tracks by shape (long straight tracks are often muons; short, thick ones are often alpha particles) and count rates over time.

### 76. Cosmic-ray muons

**Question:** How does the muon count rate change with shielding, angle or altitude?

**Method:** Build an open-hardware CosmicWatch detector, developed at MIT, which uses a plastic scintillator and silicon photomultiplier read out over USB ([Axani et al., 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.03029)). Log counts in different locations.

**Skills:** Particle detectors, counting statistics.

**Go further:** Use two detectors in coincidence to measure how the rate depends on the angle from vertical.

### 77. Radioactive decay statistics

**Question:** Do random radioactive decays follow Poisson statistics?

**Method:** Use a Geiger counter to record counts in many 10-second intervals from background radiation, or from a potassium-rich source such as a "low-sodium" salt substitute (potassium chloride). Build a histogram of the counts.

**Skills:** Probability distributions, statistics.

**Go further:** Compare the measured mean and variance; for a Poisson process they should be equal. Only use school-supplied sources under supervision.

### 78. Spectroscopy of light sources

**Question:** Can you identify elements from their emission spectra?

**Method:** Build a spectrometer from a diffraction grating and a phone camera, calibrate it with known lines (such as a fluorescent tube's mercury lines), then measure gas discharge tubes or street lights.

**Skills:** Atomic spectra, calibration.

**Go further:** Point it at a white surface lit by sunlight and identify dark Fraunhofer absorption lines.

### 79. Michelson interferometer

**Question:** Can you measure a laser's wavelength, or the thermal expansion of a metal, with interference?

**Method:** Set up a Michelson interferometer (from a school kit, or with mirrors and a beam splitter on a stable base). Move one mirror with a micrometer and count fringes passing a point.

**Skills:** Interference, precision alignment.

**Go further:** Attach a mirror to a metal rod and count fringes as it is heated to find its expansion coefficient.

### 80. Mapping magnetic fields with a Hall sensor

**Question:** How does the field of a bar magnet or coil vary with distance?

**Method:** Connect a linear Hall effect sensor to a microcontroller and move it along a ruler away from the magnet or coil.

**Skills:** Sensors, field models, programming.

**Go further:** Test whether the field far from a bar magnet falls off as $1/r^3$, as a dipole model predicts.

### 81. Magnetic levitation with feedback

**Question:** Can you stabilise a floating magnet with an electromagnet and a control loop?

**Method:** Use a Hall sensor to measure a magnet's position below an electromagnet, and a microcontroller to adjust the current through a transistor many times per second.

**Skills:** Control systems, electronics, programming.

**Go further:** Tune the control constants and plot how the magnet's position responds to a tap.

### 82. Meissner effect

**Question:** How does a superconductor interact with a magnet?

**Method:** Cool a YBCO superconductor disc with liquid nitrogen and place a small magnet above it. *Qualified supervision only:* liquid nitrogen causes severe cold burns and can displace oxygen in small rooms.

**Skills:** Superconductivity, cryogenics, safety.

**Go further:** Measure the levitation height as the disc warms to find when superconductivity stops.

### 83. Homemade seismometer

**Question:** Can you detect footsteps, traffic or distant earthquakes with a homemade seismometer?

**Method:** Suspend a mass on a spring with a magnet that moves inside a coil, and record the coil's voltage with a microcontroller or sound card.

**Skills:** Oscillators, signal processing.

**Go further:** Compare your records with nearby seismic stations' public data.

### 84. Radio astronomy: the hydrogen line

**Question:** Can you detect the 21 cm radio emission from hydrogen in the Milky Way?

**Method:** Build a horn antenna and use a low-noise amplifier and a USB software-defined radio to record the spectrum near 1420 MHz while pointing at the galactic plane.

**Skills:** Radio, spectrum analysis, astronomy.

**Go further:** Measure Doppler shifts in the hydrogen line at different galactic longitudes to infer the rotation of the galaxy.

### 85. The Sun's rotation from sunspots

**Question:** How long does the Sun take to rotate, and does it rotate faster at the equator?

**Method:** Track sunspots across several days using public images from NASA's Solar Dynamics Observatory ([SDO](https://sdo.gsfc.nasa.gov/)). Never look at the Sun directly or through optics.

**Skills:** Image analysis, spherical geometry.

**Go further:** Measure spots at different latitudes to see differential rotation.

### 86. Exoplanet transit light curves

**Question:** How big is a planet orbiting another star?

**Method:** Download a light curve from NASA's TESS mission ([mission page](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/tess/)), whose data are public through the Mikulski Archive for Space Telescopes (MAST), and measure the depth of the dip during a transit.

**Skills:** Data analysis, astronomy, programming.

**Go further:** Use the transit depth and the star's radius to estimate the planet's radius, and the time between transits to find its orbital period.

### 87. Chaos in a double pendulum

**Question:** How sensitive is a double pendulum to its starting conditions?

**Method:** Build a double pendulum from two bars and low-friction bearings. Film several releases from almost the same position, and compare with a numerical simulation.

**Skills:** Chaos, simulation, video analysis.

**Go further:** Measure how quickly two nearly identical runs diverge.

### 88. N-body orbit simulation

**Question:** Do simulated orbits obey Kepler's laws?

**Method:** Write a Python program that steps planets forward in time using Newton's law of gravitation and a stable integration method such as velocity Verlet.

**Skills:** Numerical methods, programming.

**Go further:** Check energy conservation over long runs with different time steps and integration methods.

### 89. Projectile with drag: simulation vs reality

**Question:** How much does air resistance change the path of a thrown ball?

**Method:** Simulate a projectile with quadratic drag in Python, then film real throws of light and heavy balls and compare.

**Skills:** Modelling, fitting parameters.

**Go further:** Fit the drag coefficient that best matches your video data.

### 90. Monte Carlo radioactive decay

**Question:** Does random decay of individual nuclei produce an exponential curve?

**Method:** Simulate a population of nuclei in Python or a spreadsheet, each with a fixed chance of decaying per time step, and plot the number remaining.

**Skills:** Probability, simulation, exponential models.

**Go further:** Compare your simulation with real dice-throwing or coin-flipping "decay" by your class.

### 91. Heat diffusion in a metal rod

**Question:** Does heat spread along a rod as the diffusion equation predicts?

**Method:** Heat one end of a metal rod and log temperatures at several points with thermistors and a microcontroller. Simulate the heat equation with finite differences and compare.

**Skills:** Partial differential equations, data logging.

**Go further:** Estimate the rod's thermal diffusivity from your fit.

### 92. Thermoelectric generator efficiency

**Question:** How does a Peltier module's electrical output depend on the temperature difference across it?

**Method:** Place a thermoelectric module between hot and cold water baths. Measure the temperature difference and power delivered to a load resistor.

**Skills:** Thermoelectric effect, efficiency calculations.

**Go further:** Compare the efficiency with the Carnot limit for the same temperatures.

### 93. Stirling engine performance

**Question:** How does the temperature difference across a small Stirling engine affect its speed and power?

**Method:** Run a low-temperature-difference Stirling engine on cups of water at different temperatures and measure its rotation rate.

**Skills:** Heat engines, thermodynamic cycles.

**Go further:** Compare its performance with the Carnot efficiency for each pair of temperatures.

### 94. Water rocket optimisation

**Question:** What fraction of water gives a bottle rocket the greatest height?

**Method:** Launch a rocket from a purpose-made launcher at a fixed pressure with different water volumes. Measure height with a phone altimeter app on board, or by angle measurement from a known distance. *Adult supervision*, outdoors, eye protection, and never exceed the bottle's safe pressure.

**Skills:** Momentum, thrust, experimental design.

**Go further:** Model the flight numerically and compare with your measurements.

### 95. Homemade wind tunnel

**Question:** How does shape affect drag coefficient?

**Method:** Build a small wind tunnel from a fan, a honeycomb of straws to smooth the flow and a clear test section. Measure drag on shapes using a kitchen scale or force sensor.

**Skills:** Fluid dynamics, force measurement.

**Go further:** Compare a flat plate, sphere, cylinder and teardrop shape at several speeds.

### 96. Acoustic levitation

**Question:** How does the spacing of ultrasonic standing-wave nodes compare with the wavelength?

**Method:** Build TinyLev, an open-source acoustic levitator from the University of Bristol made of 40 kHz ultrasonic transducers and a 3D-printed frame ([Marzo, Barnes and Drinkwater, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.4989995)). Levitate small polystyrene beads and measure the gap between them.

**Skills:** Standing waves, ultrasound, electronics.

**Go further:** Calculate the speed of sound from the node spacing and the transducer frequency.

### 97. Brownian motion

**Question:** Can you measure the random motion of tiny particles and relate it to temperature?

**Method:** Film diluted milk or microspheres under a microscope (a phone clip-on microscope can work). Track individual particles and calculate their mean squared displacement over time.

**Skills:** Statistical physics, particle tracking.

**Go further:** Use the Stokes–Einstein relation to estimate Boltzmann's constant from your data, and discuss your uncertainty.

### 98. Charge-to-mass ratio of the electron

**Question:** What is the ratio $e/m$ for an electron?

**Method:** Use a school fine-beam tube with Helmholtz coils. Measure the radius of the electron beam's circular path for different accelerating voltages and coil currents.

**Skills:** Magnetic forces on charges, high-voltage lab safety.

**Go further:** Plot $r^2$ against $V$ and get $e/m$ from the gradient. Compare with the CODATA value. *Teacher supervision required.*

### 99. The photoelectric effect

**Question:** How does the stopping voltage depend on light frequency?

**Method:** Use a school photoelectric apparatus with filters or LEDs of known wavelength. Measure the voltage that just stops the photocurrent for each.

**Skills:** Quantum physics, linear fits.

**Go further:** Find Planck's constant from the gradient and the work function from the intercept, and compare with project 71.

### 100. Solar cell I-V curve and temperature

**Question:** How does temperature affect a solar cell's output and efficiency?

**Method:** Sweep a variable resistor across a small solar cell under a fixed lamp and record current and voltage. Repeat with the cell warmed or cooled.

**Skills:** Semiconductor devices, maximum power point.

**Go further:** Find the maximum power point at each temperature and explain why output falls as panels heat up.

### 101. Gravitational waves from open data

**Question:** Can you find a black hole merger signal in real detector data?

**Method:** Follow the Python tutorials from the [Gravitational Wave Open Science Center](https://gwosc.org/tutorials/) to download LIGO strain data around a published event, filter it and plot a time-frequency spectrogram.

**Skills:** Signal processing, astrophysics, scientific programming.

**Go further:** Estimate the "chirp" frequency change and relate it to the masses of the merging objects.

## Writing up and presenting your project

Judges and teachers look for the same things:

- **A clear question and hypothesis,** stated at the start.
- **A method someone else could repeat,** with diagrams and a list of equipment.
- **Raw data and processed data,** in tables with units and uncertainties.
- **Graphs with labelled axes and units,** with error bars where you can estimate them.
- **An honest conclusion** that answers the question and compares your result with theory.
- **An evaluation** of weaknesses in your method and what you would improve.
- **References** for any formulas, accepted values or methods you used.

### Common mistakes

- **Too many variables changed at once,** so you cannot tell what caused the result.
- **Only one trial at each setting,** which hides random error.
- **No uncertainty estimate,** so nobody can judge whether your result agrees with theory.
- **Choosing a topic, not a question.** "Magnets" is a topic; "How does temperature affect a magnet's strength?" is a project.
- **Building something without measuring anything.** A working model is good engineering, but a physics project needs data.
