**Inflation** is a general rise in prices across the economy, which means each dollar, pound or rupee buys a little less than before. That loss of **purchasing power** is the part you feel: the same cart of groceries costs more, your raise does not stretch as far, and the money sitting in a checking account quietly shrinks in value.

In the United States, consumer prices rose **3.7%** in the 12 months to September 2026, up from about 3% a year earlier, driven mostly by energy after conflict disrupted oil shipments through the Strait of Hormuz ([BLS CPI](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cpi.nr0.htm)). Inflation is rising worldwide too: the IMF expects global consumer prices to rise **4.7%** in 2026 ([IMF, October 2026](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/weo/issues/2026/10/13/world-economic-outlook-october-2026)).

This guide explains **10 concrete ways inflation reaches your daily life**, with current figures, and what you can do about each.

**The short answer.** Inflation raises the cost of what you buy, and if your income and savings do not grow as fast, you can afford less. It hurts savers, people on fixed incomes and anyone carrying variable-rate debt. It can help people with fixed-rate debt, like a mortgage, because they repay with cheaper dollars. The most useful defenses are a budget that tracks your own price changes, getting a real return on savings, avoiding high-interest debt, and asking for raises that at least match inflation.

**How we wrote this.** Figures are from official U.S. sources (Bureau of Labor Statistics, Bureau of Economic Analysis, Federal Reserve, Energy Information Administration and Social Security Administration) and the IMF, checked as of 1 November 2026. Worked examples are our own calculations, with assumptions stated. This is general education, **not personal financial advice**.

## Inflation in one minute

- **How it is measured.** The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics tracks the prices of a large "basket" of goods and services that households buy, from rent and gasoline to haircuts and streaming services. The change in that basket's cost is the **Consumer Price Index (CPI)**.
- **Headline vs. core.** *Headline* inflation includes everything. *Core* inflation leaves out food and energy, whose prices swing a lot, to show the underlying trend. In September 2026, headline was 3.7% and core was **2.6%**, which tells you energy did most of the damage.
- **The target.** The Federal Reserve aims for 2% inflation over the longer run, measured by the personal consumption expenditures (PCE) price index ([Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/faqs/economy_14400.htm)). PCE inflation was 3.6% in September 2026 ([BEA](https://www.bea.gov/data/personal-consumption-expenditures-price-index)).
- **Your rate is personal.** The CPI is an average. A commuter who drives 40 miles a day and heats with oil has had a far higher personal inflation rate in 2026 than a renter who walks to work.

## How inflation hits your spending

### 1. Fuel, heating and travel cost more

Energy is the clearest example of inflation in 2026. Energy prices in the CPI were up **20.4%** over the year to September, with gasoline up 34.2% and heating oil up 74.2%. Gasoline alone accounted for more than half of the 0.6% rise in prices in September ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cpi.nr0.htm)). On 26 October 2026, regular gasoline averaged about **\$4.27 a gallon**, compared with about \$3.04 a year earlier ([EIA](https://www.eia.gov/petroleum/gasdiesel/)).

**What it means for you.** A driver who uses 50 gallons a month is paying roughly **\$60 more a month** than a year ago (50 × \$1.23). Higher fuel costs also spread to other prices, because almost everything is delivered by truck: airline fares were up 22.4%.

**What you can do.** Combine trips, compare fuel prices with an app, check tire pressure, and look at whether a monthly transit pass now beats driving. Book flights earlier and be flexible with dates.

### 2. Groceries and eating out

Food prices rose more slowly than energy in 2026: **1.8%** for groceries and **3.5%** for restaurant meals. Restaurant prices usually rise faster because they include wages and rent, not just ingredients. Averages hide big swings in individual items: eggs fell sharply in 2026 after a bird flu shortage eased, while chocolate stayed expensive after cocoa prices hit records ([20 real-world examples of supply and demand](/articles/supply-and-demand-examples)).

Global food prices are rising again. The UN Food and Agriculture Organization's Food Price Index was **5.8% higher** in September 2026 than a year earlier, led by cereals and sugar ([FAO](https://www.fao.org/worldfoodsituation/foodpricesindex/en/)). Because food is a larger share of spending for lower-income households, and in lower-income countries, food inflation hurts them most.

**What you can do.** Buy store brands, plan meals around what is on sale and in season, and compare prices per unit (per ounce or per 100 grams) rather than per package.

### 3. Shrinkflation: smaller packs at the same price

Sometimes the price stays the same but you get less: a chocolate bar shrinks from 1.6 to 1.5 ounces, or a cereal box loses a few servings. This is known as **shrinkflation**. The BLS records the new size and counts the higher price per ounce as a price increase, so shrinkflation is captured in the CPI. Its research found that product downsizing added only about **0.01 percentage points a year** to overall inflation from 2015 to 2019, though it mattered more in some categories ([BLS Beyond the Numbers, 2023](https://www.bls.gov/opub/btn/volume-12/measuring-shrinkflation-and-its-impact-on-inflation.htm)).

**What you can do.** The unit price on the shelf label is the honest number. Compare it, not the package price.

### 4. Housing costs

Shelter, which includes rent and the estimated rental value of owned homes, makes up about a **third** of the CPI basket. Shelter costs rose **3.2%** over the year to September 2026, slower than overall inflation, partly because a wave of new apartment buildings gave renters more choice ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cpi.nr0.htm)). But housing is where inflation is hardest to escape, because you cannot easily cut back.

**What you can do.** If you rent, compare your renewal offer with current listings nearby; landlords often prefer a small discount to an empty unit. If you own, check your home insurance and property tax assessments, which often rise with inflation.

## How inflation hits your income and savings

### 5. Your paycheck buys less

Inflation matters most when prices rise faster than pay. In September 2026, average hourly earnings in the U.S. were up **3.0%** over the year ([BLS Employment Situation](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/empsit.nr0.htm)). With prices up 3.7%, **real** (inflation-adjusted) hourly earnings fell **0.7%** ([BLS Real Earnings](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/realer.nr0.htm)). In other words, the average worker got a raise but can buy slightly less than a year ago.

**A quick test.** Subtract the inflation rate from your raise. A 3% raise with 3.7% inflation is a real pay cut of about 0.7%. To keep your purchasing power, your raise needs to at least match inflation.

**What you can do.** Use the current inflation rate in pay conversations, and check what similar jobs pay. Building in-demand skills is the most durable defense, because pay for skills in short supply tends to keep up better.

### 6. Cash savings lose value

Money in a checking account paying close to 0% loses purchasing power at the full inflation rate. At 3.7% inflation, **\$10,000** left in cash for a year will buy what about **\$9,640** bought a year earlier (10,000 ÷ 1.037).

The number to watch is the **real interest rate**: your savings rate minus inflation. A savings account paying 4% when inflation is 3.7% earns a real return of only about 0.3%, before tax. One paying 0.5% loses about 3.2% a year in real terms.

**What you can do.** Keep your emergency fund in an insured high-yield savings account or money market fund rather than a zero-interest account. For U.S. savers, Treasury inflation-protected securities (TIPS) and Series I savings bonds are designed to keep pace with CPI; check current rates at [TreasuryDirect](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/savings-bonds/i-bonds/i-bonds-interest-rates/).

### 7. Fixed incomes and benefits

People living on a fixed pension or annuity that is not adjusted for inflation lose purchasing power every year. Over 20 years of 3% inflation, a fixed \$2,000 monthly pension buys what about \$1,100 buys today (2,000 ÷ 1.03²⁰).

Some income is **indexed** to inflation. U.S. Social Security benefits are adjusted each year using the CPI-W, a version of the CPI for urban wage earners, comparing July through September with a year earlier. On 14 October 2026, the Social Security Administration announced a **3.6%** cost-of-living adjustment for 2027, about \$75 a month for the average retiree, starting in January ([SSA](https://www.ssa.gov/blog/en/posts/2026-10-14.html)). The 2026 adjustment was 2.8%. The adjustment arrives months after the price rises it compensates for, so retirees feel inflation before they get relief.

**What you can do.** If you are planning retirement, check which parts of your income are indexed and which are not, and keep some long-term savings in assets that have historically grown faster than inflation.

## How inflation hits borrowing and investing

### 8. Borrowing gets more expensive

Central banks fight inflation by raising interest rates, which makes borrowing more expensive and slows spending. The Federal Reserve **raised** its policy rate in September 2026 to a range of 3.75% to 4.00% and held it there in October, saying "inflation remains elevated" ([Federal Reserve, 28 October 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20261028a.htm)).

Those decisions flow through to what you pay:

- **Mortgages.** The average 30-year fixed mortgage rate was **7.24%** in the week ending 29 October 2026, according to Freddie Mac ([FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/MORTGAGE30US)), up from about 6.2% a year earlier.
- **Credit cards.** The average interest rate on credit card accounts was **21.19%** in August 2026, and 22.36% on accounts that were charged interest ([Federal Reserve G.19](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/g19/current/default.htm)).
- **Variable-rate loans.** Adjustable-rate mortgages, home equity lines and many student and business loans reset as rates rise.

**What you can do.** Pay down variable-rate and credit card debt first. If you are shopping for a loan, compare several lenders; small differences in rate add up to large sums over a mortgage.

### 9. Fixed-rate debt gets lighter

Inflation has winners. If you have a **fixed-rate** mortgage or loan, your monthly payment stays the same while prices and, usually, wages rise. Over time, the payment takes up a smaller share of your income, and you repay the lender with dollars that buy less than the ones you borrowed. This is why long periods of inflation tend to shift wealth from lenders and savers to borrowers with fixed-rate debt.

**What you can do.** If you already have a low fixed-rate mortgage, there is usually no rush to pay it off early during high inflation, as long as you are saving and investing elsewhere. This does not apply to credit cards, which carry high and usually variable rates.

### 10. Investment returns and long-term goals

Inflation changes what a "good" return means. A 5% return with 3.7% inflation is a real return of about 1.3%. Over decades, the gap matters: \$100,000 growing at 5% for 30 years becomes about \$432,000, but with 3% inflation that is worth about \$178,000 in today's money.

Inflation also changes long-term targets. A retirement goal set in today's dollars needs to rise with prices each year, or you will save too little. Historically, broadly diversified stocks have outpaced inflation over long periods, while cash and many bonds have struggled to keep up in high-inflation years, though past results never guarantee future ones.

**What you can do.** Plan in real terms: set goals in today's money and assume a realistic inflation rate. Keep short-term money in safe assets and long-term money in a diversified, low-cost mix suited to your time horizon. ## Track your own inflation rate

The CPI is an average for the whole country. To see how inflation affects **you**:

1. **Compare last year's spending with this year's** for your biggest categories: housing, transport, food, utilities, insurance and debt payments.
2. **Work out your personal rate.** Add up what the same basket of spending cost then and now, and divide: (this year ÷ last year) − 1.
3. **Find the biggest driver.** In 2026, that is fuel for most drivers. Focus your effort on the category that moved most.
4. **Check your income against it.** If your pay or benefits grew more slowly than your personal rate, your purchasing power fell.

A simple budget makes this much easier; our guide to [budgeting methods](/articles/budgeting-methods) compares several ways to set one up. ## FAQ

### What is inflation in simple words?

Inflation is when prices across the economy rise over time, so the same amount of money buys less. If inflation is 3.7%, something that cost \$100 a year ago costs about \$103.70 today on average.

### Who is hurt most by inflation?

People whose income or savings do not keep up: those on fixed pensions, savers with money in low-interest accounts, workers without bargaining power, and households that spend a large share of income on food and energy. People with variable-rate debt are also hurt when interest rates rise.

### Does inflation ever help anyone?

Yes. Borrowers with fixed-rate debt repay with money worth less than when they borrowed. Owners of assets whose value rises with prices, such as some property and businesses, can also come out ahead. Governments with large debts benefit in a similar way.

### Will prices go back down when inflation falls?

Usually not. Lower inflation means prices rise more slowly, not that they fall. Individual items can get cheaper, as eggs did in 2026, but a broad fall in prices (deflation) is rare and often a sign of a weak economy. The realistic goal is for incomes to catch up with prices.
