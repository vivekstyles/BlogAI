Economics has its own vocabulary, and news about the economy assumes you already know it. Words like *elasticity*, *real interest rate*, *stagflation* or *tariff pass-through* can make a simple story sound complicated. Once you know what they mean, most economic news becomes much easier to follow, and so do many money decisions.

This guide explains **30 economic concepts every beginner should know**, each in plain language with an example from 2025 or 2026. It is built as a glossary you can scan or read in order.

**The short answer.** Learn how **markets** set prices and where they go wrong, how **money and interest rates** work, how the **whole economy** grows and shrinks, and the **global forces** shaping 2026: an energy price shock, tariffs, fragile supply chains and a boom in AI investment. The 30 concepts below cover each of these.

**How we wrote this.** This glossary complements our [15 basic economic principles](/articles/basic-economic-principles), which covers the foundations (scarcity, opportunity cost, incentives, marginal thinking, comparative advantage and supply and demand), so we do not repeat those here. Current examples are tied to official data or published research, cited in place and listed at the end, and checked as of 1 November 2026.

## Markets and business

### 1. Microeconomics vs. macroeconomics

**Microeconomics** studies individual decisions: how a household chooses what to buy, how a firm sets its prices, how a single market works. **Macroeconomics** studies the economy as a whole: total output, unemployment, inflation and interest rates.

**Example.** Why the price of eggs fell in 2026 is a micro question (supply recovered after bird flu). Why overall U.S. prices rose 3.7% over the year to September 2026 is a macro question ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cpi.nr0.htm)).

### 2. Elasticity

**Elasticity** measures how strongly buyers or sellers react to a change in price. Demand is **elastic** when a small price rise causes a big fall in purchases (one brand of cereal among many). It is **inelastic** when people keep buying anyway (gasoline, insulin, electricity).

**Example.** Gasoline prices in the U.S. rose 34.2% over the year to September 2026, yet most people kept driving to work. Inelastic demand is why supply shocks in energy cause such large price swings. We give more examples in [20 real-world examples of supply and demand](/articles/supply-and-demand-examples).

### 3. Consumer and producer surplus

**Consumer surplus** is the difference between what you would be willing to pay and what you actually pay. **Producer surplus** is the difference between the price a seller gets and the lowest price they would accept. Together, they measure the total benefit a market creates.

**Example.** If you would pay \$30 for a concert ticket and it costs \$20, you gain \$10 of consumer surplus. Dynamic pricing, like that used for 2026 World Cup tickets, tries to capture more of that surplus for the seller.

### 4. Market structure: competition, monopoly and oligopoly

Markets range from **perfect competition** (many small sellers of identical products, such as wheat farmers), through **monopolistic competition** (many sellers of slightly different products, such as restaurants) and **oligopoly** (a few large firms, such as airlines or mobile networks), to **monopoly** (one seller).

**Why it matters.** With fewer competitors, firms have more power to raise prices above their costs. Competition authorities, such as the U.S. Federal Trade Commission and the UK's Competition and Markets Authority, exist to prevent abuse of that power.

### 5. Economies of scale

**Economies of scale** happen when the average cost of making each unit falls as a firm produces more, because fixed costs like factories, research or software are spread over more units.

**Example.** Training a large AI model costs a huge amount, but once it exists, serving one more user costs very little. This helps explain why a few very large firms dominate AI and cloud computing.

### 6. Network effects

A product has **network effects** when it becomes more useful as more people use it. Phones, messaging apps, payment systems and marketplaces all work this way.

**Why it matters.** Network effects can make markets "winner takes most," because users flock to the biggest network. That makes it hard for new competitors to break in, even with a better product.

### 7. Price discrimination and dynamic pricing

**Price discrimination** means charging different customers different prices for the same thing, based on what they are willing to pay. Student discounts, senior fares and off-peak tickets are common forms. **Dynamic pricing** changes prices in real time as demand changes, as ride-hailing apps and airlines do.

**Example.** FIFA used dynamic pricing for 2026 World Cup tickets for the first time, with group-stage seats starting at \$60 and high-demand matches priced far higher ([ESPN](https://www.espn.com/soccer/story/_/id/46147005/2026-world-cup-tickets-fifa-dynamic-pricing-prices)).

### 8. Information asymmetry

**Information asymmetry** exists when one side of a deal knows more than the other. It leads to two classic problems:

- **Adverse selection**: the people most eager to buy insurance are often those most likely to claim, and the cars most likely to be sold used are often the ones with hidden problems. George Akerlof's 1970 paper "The Market for 'Lemons'" showed that this can make a market shrink or collapse.
- **Moral hazard**: people take more risk once they are protected from the cost, such as driving less carefully when fully insured.

**Why it matters.** Warranties, inspections, credit scores, reviews and deductibles all exist to reduce these problems.

### 9. Behavioral economics

**Behavioral economics** studies how real people depart from the purely rational decision-maker of textbook models. Key findings include **loss aversion** (losses hurt more than equal gains please, a central idea in Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky's 1979 prospect theory), **present bias** (we overvalue now versus later) and the power of **defaults**.

**Example.** When employers automatically enroll workers in retirement plans, far more workers save, even though they could easily opt out. Madrian and Shea's 2001 study of one U.S. company is the classic evidence. Our [15 personal finance rules](/articles/personal-finance-rules) uses this research.

### 10. Game theory

**Game theory** studies decisions where the outcome for each person depends on what others do. A **Nash equilibrium**, named after mathematician John Nash, is a situation where no one can do better by changing their own choice alone.

**Example.** Two petrol stations on the same corner each know that if they raise prices, the other may not follow and will steal their customers. This kind of reasoning explains price wars, arms races and trade wars, where each side's best response can leave both worse off.

## Money and finance

### 11. Money and its three jobs

**Money** is anything widely accepted in exchange for goods and services. It does three jobs: a **medium of exchange** (you can buy things with it), a **unit of account** (prices are quoted in it) and a **store of value** (it holds value over time). Most modern money is created by commercial banks when they make loans, not printed by governments.

**Why it matters.** Inflation weakens money's job as a store of value, which is why people look for other assets in high-inflation periods. Debates over cryptocurrencies are often about whether they can do all three jobs reliably.

### 12. Nominal vs. real values

A **nominal** value is measured in today's money. A **real** value is adjusted for inflation, so it measures actual purchasing power.

**Example.** U.S. average hourly earnings rose 3.0% over the year to September 2026 (nominal). After 3.7% inflation, **real** hourly earnings fell 0.7% ([BLS Real Earnings](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/realer.nr0.htm)). The same applies to interest rates: a **real interest rate** is roughly the nominal rate minus inflation.

### 13. Compound interest and the time value of money

**Compound interest** means earning interest on your interest, so money grows faster over time. The **time value of money** is the idea that a dollar today is worth more than a dollar in the future, because today's dollar can be invested and because of inflation.

**Example.** \$1,000 invested at 7% a year becomes about \$1,967 in 10 years and \$7,612 in 30 years. The same compounding works against you on debt: the average U.S. credit card rate was **21.19%** in August 2026 ([Federal Reserve G.19](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/g19/current/default.htm)).

### 14. Central banks and monetary policy

A **central bank**, such as the U.S. Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank or the Reserve Bank of India, manages a country's money and interest rates. **Monetary policy** is the use of interest rates (and sometimes asset purchases) to keep inflation low and the economy stable.

**Example.** The Federal Reserve raised its policy rate to **3.75%–4.00%** in September 2026 and held it in October, saying "inflation remains elevated" ([Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20261028a.htm)). Higher rates are meant to cool spending and bring inflation back toward its 2% target.

### 15. Inflation, deflation and stagflation

- **Inflation** is a general rise in prices.
- **Disinflation** is when inflation slows but prices are still rising.
- **Deflation** is a general fall in prices, which can be harmful because people delay spending and debts become heavier.
- **Stagflation** is high inflation combined with weak growth and rising unemployment, as many countries saw after the oil shocks of the 1970s.

**Example.** In 2026, energy prices jumped while U.S. job growth slowed sharply (29,000 jobs in September), the mix that makes economists watch for stagflation. But GDP still grew at a 3.4% annualized rate in the third quarter ([BEA](https://www.bea.gov/data/gdp/gross-domestic-product)), so the label does not fit the U.S. data so far. See [10 ways inflation affects your daily life](/articles/how-inflation-affects-daily-life) for what inflation means for your budget.

### 16. Bonds and yields

A **bond** is a loan to a government or company that pays interest and returns the original amount at a set date. The **yield** is the return you get if you buy the bond at today's market price. Bond prices and yields move in opposite directions: when interest rates rise, existing bonds paying lower rates become less valuable.

**Why it matters.** Government bond yields set the base for mortgage rates, corporate borrowing costs and savings rates. The average 30-year U.S. mortgage rate was **7.24%** in late October 2026 ([FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/MORTGAGE30US)), closely following long-term Treasury yields.

### 17. Risk, return and diversification

Investors usually demand a higher expected **return** for taking more **risk**. **Diversification**, spreading money across many different investments, reduces the risk that one bad outcome wipes you out, without necessarily lowering expected returns.

**Example.** In early 2026, gold prices set records and then fell back, and memory-chip prices nearly doubled in a single quarter. An investor concentrated in any one asset or industry feels such swings in full; a diversified portfolio is less exposed to each of them. The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission explains the basics at [Investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/getting-started/asset-allocation).

### 18. Exchange rates and purchasing power parity

An **exchange rate** is the price of one currency in another. **Purchasing power parity (PPP)** is the idea that, over the long run, exchange rates should adjust so that the same basket of goods costs the same everywhere. In practice, they often do not, which is why things feel cheap or expensive when you travel.

**Why it matters.** International comparisons of income use PPP-adjusted figures, because \$1 converted at market exchange rates buys far more in India or Vietnam than in Switzerland.

## The whole economy

### 19. Gross domestic product (GDP)

**GDP** is the total value of goods and services produced in a country. **Nominal GDP** uses current prices; **real GDP** adjusts for inflation and shows whether output actually grew.

**Example.** The IMF expects the world economy to grow **3.1%** in 2026, down from 3.5% in 2025, mainly because of the energy shock ([IMF, October 2026](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/weo/issues/2026/10/13/world-economic-outlook-october-2026)). We explain GDP and 24 other measures in [25 economic indicators that explain the global economy](/articles/economic-indicators-explained).

### 20. The business cycle and recessions

The **business cycle** is the pattern of expansion and contraction around an economy's long-run growth path. A **recession** is a significant, widespread decline in economic activity lasting more than a few months. A common shorthand is two quarters in a row of falling real GDP, but in the U.S. the official dates are set by the National Bureau of Economic Research's Business Cycle Dating Committee, which looks at jobs, income, spending and production.

**Why it matters.** Recessions raise unemployment and cut incomes. Governments and central banks try to soften them, which is the job of the next few concepts.

### 21. Types of unemployment

- **Frictional unemployment**: people between jobs or just entering the job market. Some is normal and healthy.
- **Structural unemployment**: a mismatch between workers' skills or locations and available jobs, for example when automation changes what employers need.
- **Cyclical unemployment**: job losses caused by a downturn in the business cycle.

**Example.** The U.S. unemployment rate was **4.2%** in September 2026 ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/empsit.nr0.htm)). Debates about AI and jobs are mostly about whether it will create structural unemployment in some occupations, and how quickly workers can retrain.

### 22. Fiscal policy

**Fiscal policy** is the government's use of taxes and spending to influence the economy. Cutting taxes or raising spending tends to boost demand in a downturn; raising taxes or cutting spending cools it. The **multiplier** describes how much total output changes for each dollar of government spending or tax change, and economists disagree about its size.

**Example.** During the 2026 energy shock, the IMF has advised governments to use "temporary, targeted" income support for the most vulnerable rather than broad measures that hold prices down, which it says can cost 3 to 22 times more ([IMF, October 2026](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/weo/issues/2026/10/13/world-economic-outlook-october-2026)).

### 23. Government debt and deficits

A **budget deficit** is the gap when a government spends more than it collects in a year. **Government debt** is the total accumulated borrowing. Both are usually measured as a percentage of GDP.

**Example.** The IMF estimates global public debt at just under **94% of world GDP** in 2025 and expects it to reach 100% by 2029 ([IMF Fiscal Monitor, April 2026](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/FM/Issues/2026/04/15/fiscal-monitor-april-2026)). High debt is easier to carry when interest rates are low relative to growth, and harder when rates rise, as they did in 2026.

### 24. Human capital and productivity

**Human capital** is the knowledge, skills and health that make people productive. **Productivity** is output per hour of work. Together with physical capital (machines, buildings) and technology, they determine how much an economy can produce and how high wages can go.

**Why it matters.** Education and training are investments in human capital, which is why they tend to raise lifetime earnings on average. In October 2026, the Federal Reserve described U.S. productivity growth as "strong" and capital investment as "robust."

### 25. Supply shocks

A **supply shock** is a sudden change in an economy's ability to produce, such as a war, pandemic, drought or a key input becoming scarce. A negative supply shock raises prices and lowers output at the same time, which puts central banks in a bind: raising rates fights inflation but deepens the slowdown.

**Example.** The disruption to oil shipments through the Strait of Hormuz in 2026 is a textbook negative supply shock. U.S. energy prices were up **20.4%** over the year to September ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cpi.nr0.htm)), and the U.S. Energy Information Administration expects Brent crude to average \$96 a barrel in 2026 ([EIA](https://www.eia.gov/outlooks/steo/)).

## Global and 2026 themes

### 26. Trade, globalization and the trade balance

**Globalization** is the growing connection of economies through trade, investment, migration and information. The **trade balance** is a country's exports minus its imports. A **trade deficit** means a country imports more than it exports.

**Why it matters.** A trade deficit is not automatically bad: it often reflects a country investing more than it saves, financed by foreign investors. The World Trade Organization expects world goods trade to grow **3.9%** in 2026 despite the conflict in the Middle East ([WTO, 8 October 2026](https://www.wto.org/english/news_e/news26_e/stat_08oct26_510_e.htm)).

### 27. Tariffs and who pays them

A **tariff** is a tax on imported goods. It is paid to the government by the importing business, which may pass some or all of the cost on to customers. How much is passed on is called **pass-through**.

**Example.** Studies of the U.S. tariffs imposed in 2018 and 2019 found that nearly the full cost was passed on to U.S. importers and consumers rather than absorbed by foreign exporters, including work by Mary Amiti, Stephen Redding and David Weinstein published in the *Journal of Economic Perspectives* in 2019. Pass-through can differ by product and over time, and economists continue to study the tariffs introduced since 2025.

### 28. Supply chains and resilience

A **supply chain** is the network of firms, factories and transport links that turns raw materials into finished goods. Decades of globalization made supply chains cheaper but sometimes fragile, with key parts made in only one place.

**Example.** The chip shortage of 2021, the COVID-19 disruptions and the 2026 energy shock pushed many companies and governments to favor **resilience** over the lowest cost: holding more inventory, using several suppliers, and moving production closer to home or to friendly countries (**nearshoring** and **friendshoring**). The trade-off is higher costs in normal times.

### 29. AI and the economy

**Artificial intelligence** affects the economy in two ways. In the short run, building it is a huge **investment** boom: data centers, chips and power plants. In the long run, the question is whether it raises **productivity**, and how its gains and job disruptions are shared.

**Example.** The WTO found that AI-related goods such as semiconductors and servers accounted for **47%** of the growth in world goods trade in the first half of 2026 ([WTO](https://www.wto.org/english/news_e/news26_e/stat_08oct26_510_e.htm)). The IMF credits AI investment with helping to hold up global growth, but also lists "a reassessment of AI expectations" as a key risk. Demand from AI data centers has also pushed up prices of memory chips and contributed to rising electricity demand, as we explain in our [supply and demand examples](/articles/supply-and-demand-examples). For how AI is changing jobs, see [AI and the future of work](/articles/ai-future-of-work-examples).

### 30. Inequality and the Gini coefficient

**Inequality** describes how unevenly income or wealth is shared. The most common measure is the **Gini coefficient**, which runs from 0 (everyone has the same) to 1, or 100 (one person has everything). The World Bank publishes Gini estimates for most countries ([World Bank](https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/SI.POV.GINI)).

**Why it matters.** Average figures like GDP per person can rise while most households see little gain. Inflation can also widen inequality, because lower-income households spend a larger share of their budget on food and energy, the prices that rose fastest in 2026.

## How to keep learning

1. **Follow one indicator a month.** Read the official release for CPI, jobs or GDP when it comes out, and connect it to the concepts above.
2. **Ask "real or nominal?" and "short run or long run?"** Those two questions clear up most confusing economic claims.
3. **Read people who explain their reasoning.** Our directory lists [Personal Finance blogs](/category/personal-finance) that cover markets and the economy.
4. **Apply the ideas to your own money.** Concepts like compound interest, real returns and diversification matter most when you use them; our [money management tips](/articles/money-management-tips) are a practical place to start.

## FAQ

### What are the basic concepts of economics?

The foundations are scarcity, opportunity cost, incentives, supply and demand, and trade, which we cover in [15 basic economic principles](/articles/basic-economic-principles). Beyond those, beginners should know elasticity, market structure, inflation, interest rates, GDP, unemployment, and monetary and fiscal policy.

### What is the difference between monetary and fiscal policy?

Monetary policy is run by a central bank, which sets interest rates and controls the money supply. Fiscal policy is run by the government, through taxes and spending. Both aim to keep the economy stable, but they use different tools and are decided by different institutions.

### What economic concepts matter most in 2026?

The big themes of 2026 are the energy supply shock and its effect on inflation, central banks raising rates again, high government debt, tariffs and trade, and the economic impact of AI investment. Concepts 15 and 25 to 29 above explain each of these.

### How can a beginner learn economics?

Start with a short list of concepts like this one, then follow real data releases and news with those concepts in mind. Free courses from universities and central bank education pages are good next steps, and a standard introductory textbook covers everything here in more depth.
