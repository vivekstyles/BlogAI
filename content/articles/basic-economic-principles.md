An **economic principle** is a short rule that explains how people, businesses and governments make choices when resources are limited. You do not need equations to use them. They explain everyday puzzles: why concert tickets cost more on a Saturday, why a free-trade deal can help both countries, why prices rise when a central bank creates too much money, and why a well-meant price cap can leave shelves empty.

This guide covers **15 principles that do most of the work** in introductory economics. Each comes with a plain explanation, a real example, and **where it stops working**, so you can use it without over-applying it.

**The short answer.** Resources are scarce, so every choice has a cost (what you give up). People respond to incentives and decide at the margin. Trade and markets usually create value, but not always, and governments can sometimes fix what markets get wrong. Over the long run, living standards depend on productivity, and inflation depends on how much money is created relative to what the economy produces.

**How we wrote this.** The list follows the standard core of introductory economics, including N. Gregory Mankiw's well-known "ten principles of economics" from his *Principles of Economics* textbook, plus five more that come up constantly in everyday decisions. Examples are tied to published research or official data, cited in place and listed at the end, and current figures are checked as of 1 November 2026. This is general education, not financial advice.

## How individuals make decisions

### 1. Scarcity: you cannot have everything

**The principle.** Time, money, land, labor and attention are limited, while wants are not. Economics starts from this gap. Every person, company and government has to decide what to do with what it has, and choosing one thing means not choosing another.

**Example.** A household with \$500 left at the end of the month can pay down a credit card, add to savings, or take a weekend trip. It cannot do all three with the same \$500. A government faces the same problem at a larger scale: money spent on defense cannot also be spent on schools unless it raises taxes or borrows, which creates its own costs.

**Where it stops.** Scarcity does not mean everything is fixed. Technology and trade can stretch what a fixed amount of resources produces, which is why later principles about productivity and trade matter.

### 2. Opportunity cost: the real cost is what you give up

**The principle.** The cost of a choice is the value of the **best alternative you give up**, not just the money you pay. Economists call this the opportunity cost.

**Example.** The cost of a full-time, two-year master's degree is not only tuition. It also includes two years of salary you would have earned. If tuition is \$40,000 and you would have earned \$55,000 a year, the opportunity cost is about \$150,000 before taxes, which is the number to compare with the higher pay the degree may bring.

**Where it stops.** Opportunity cost is hard to measure when the alternatives are not priced, such as time with family or health. The principle still helps: it forces you to name what you are giving up, even when you cannot put a number on it.

### 3. Rational people think at the margin

**The principle.** Most decisions are not all-or-nothing. They are about **one more** or **one less**: one more hour of work, one more employee, one more slice of pizza. A good decision compares the extra benefit of the next unit with its extra cost. This is called *marginal* thinking.

**Example.** An airline with an empty seat 30 minutes before departure may sell it for far less than the average cost per seat. The fuel, crew and plane are paid for whether or not the seat is filled. The extra cost of one more passenger is a snack and a little fuel, so any fare above that adds profit.

**Where it stops.** People do not always calculate this carefully. Behavioral economics shows that habits, defaults and mental shortcuts often drive choices. Marginal thinking describes what a careful decision looks like, not always what people actually do.

### 4. People respond to incentives

**The principle.** When the costs or rewards of an action change, behavior changes. A higher price discourages buying; a higher wage encourages working; a tax or fine discourages the activity it is placed on.

**Example.** In October 2015, England required large retailers to charge for single-use plastic carrier bags. The UK government reports that the number of these bags sold by the main retailers has fallen by **98%** since the charge was introduced, more than 7.5 billion fewer bags a year ([GOV.UK, 2025 to 2026 data](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/carrier-bag-charge-summary-of-data-in-england/single-use-plastic-carrier-bags-charge-data-for-england-2025-to-2026)). A small charge changed a habit that had seemed fixed.

**Where it stops.** Incentives can backfire when people find a way around them or when a payment replaces a moral reason to act. The figure above covers the main retailers only, and the government's data shows smaller retailers still sell hundreds of millions of bags. Always ask how people might respond that the designer did not intend (see principle 12).

### 5. Ignore sunk costs

**The principle.** A **sunk cost** is money or time already spent that you cannot get back. Because it is gone whatever you do next, it should not affect the next decision. Only future costs and benefits matter.

**Example.** You paid \$80 for a concert ticket, then you get sick on the day. Going will not bring back the \$80. The only question is whether the concert is worth the effort of going while sick. Businesses face the same trap when they keep funding a failing project because "we have already spent so much on it."

**Where it stops.** Psychologists have shown that people find this hard; the error is known as the *sunk cost fallacy*. Arkes and Ayton's 1999 review in *Psychological Bulletin* discusses why adults fall for it more often than they should. Past spending can still carry useful information (it may tell you how much the next stage will really cost), but it is not a reason in itself.

### 6. Diminishing returns

**The principle.** When you add more of one input while others stay fixed, each extra unit usually adds **less** than the one before. The same idea applies to enjoyment: the second slice of cake is usually less satisfying than the first. Economists call this **diminishing marginal returns** (for production) and **diminishing marginal utility** (for satisfaction).

**Example.** A café with one espresso machine can add a second barista and serve many more customers. A fifth barista at the same machine adds very little, because they spend most of their time waiting.

**Where it stops.** Some activities show *increasing* returns for a while, especially software and networks, where the cost of serving one more user is close to zero and each user makes the product more useful to others. Diminishing returns is the usual pattern, not a law of nature.

## How people interact in markets

### 7. Trade can make everyone better off

**The principle.** A voluntary exchange happens only when both sides expect to gain. Trade lets people and countries specialize in what they do well and buy the rest, so the total amount produced and consumed rises.

**Example.** Few people grow their own food, make their own phone, and write their own software. By specializing in one job and trading for everything else, almost everyone ends up with far more than they could make alone.

**Where it stops.** Trade can make a country better off **on average** while some workers and regions lose, for example when a factory closes because imports are cheaper. Whether those losers are helped through retraining, social insurance or other policy is a political choice, and ignoring them has real costs.

### 8. Comparative advantage

**The principle.** You should specialize in what you produce at the **lowest opportunity cost**, even if someone else is better at everything. David Ricardo set out this idea in 1817, and it is one of the least intuitive and most important results in economics.

**Example.** A lawyer may type faster than their assistant. But an hour of the lawyer's time spent typing is an hour not spent on legal work billed at \$300. The assistant's opportunity cost of typing is far lower, so both are better off when the assistant types and the lawyer practices law. Countries gain from trade in the same way.

**Where it stops.** The theory assumes workers and capital can move between industries without much friction, which is not always true in practice. It also says nothing about national security or the risk of depending on a single supplier, which governments weigh separately.

### 9. Prices are signals that coordinate the economy

**The principle.** No single person knows how much coffee is grown in Brazil, how many truck drivers are available, or how much people in your town want avocados. Prices bring this information together. A rising price tells buyers to use less and producers to make more, without anyone giving orders. Friedrich Hayek made this argument in his 1945 essay "The Use of Knowledge in Society."

**Example.** When the Strait of Hormuz was disrupted in 2026, oil prices rose sharply. The U.S. Energy Information Administration's October forecast puts Brent crude at an average of \$96 a barrel for 2026 ([EIA Short-Term Energy Outlook](https://www.eia.gov/outlooks/steo/), 6 October 2026). Higher prices pushed airlines, shippers and drivers to economize, and rewarded producers elsewhere for pumping and shipping more, all without a central plan.

**Where it stops.** Prices only signal well when they are allowed to move and when they include all the costs of an activity. When they do not, see principle 11.

### 10. Supply and demand set prices

**The principle.** In a competitive market, the price moves until the amount buyers want equals the amount sellers want to sell. If supply falls or demand rises, the price goes up; if supply rises or demand falls, the price goes down.

**Example.** U.S. egg prices spiked in early 2025 after bird flu killed millions of laying hens, then fell sharply as flocks recovered. The USDA's May 2026 *Livestock, Dairy, and Poultry Outlook* put the average retail price at \$2.25 a dozen in April 2026, about 62% below a year earlier ([The Poultry Site](https://www.thepoultrysite.com/news/2026/06/us-egg-prices-fall-sharply-as-flock-rebuilds-after-hpai-losses)). Nothing about breakfast habits changed much; the supply did.

We cover 20 more cases in [real-world examples of supply and demand](/articles/supply-and-demand-examples).

**Where it stops.** The basic model assumes many buyers and sellers. When a few firms dominate a market, or when buyers cannot compare products easily, prices can stay above the competitive level.

### 11. Markets can fail, and governments can sometimes help

**The principle.** Markets work poorly in a few well-known situations:

- **Externalities.** A cost or benefit falls on people outside the deal, such as pollution from a factory or the wider health benefit of a vaccine.
- **Public goods.** Things like street lighting or national defense benefit everyone, and it is hard to charge only those who use them, so markets supply too little.
- **Market power.** A monopoly can raise prices above what competition would allow.
- **Missing information.** When one side knows much more than the other, as with used cars or health insurance, markets can break down.

In these cases, taxes, regulation, public provision or better disclosure can improve outcomes.

**Example.** The plastic bag charge in principle 4 is a small tax on an externality: litter and waste that the shopper does not pay for at the till.

**Where it stops.** Governments can fail too. Policies may be captured by interest groups, poorly designed, or more costly than the problem they solve. "The market failed" is a reason to consider a fix, not proof that any particular fix will work.

### 12. Watch for unintended consequences

**The principle.** People react to rules in ways rule-makers do not always expect. A good policy analysis asks "and then what?" more than once.

**Example.** Rent control aims to protect tenants. A study of San Francisco's 1994 expansion of rent control by Rebecca Diamond, Tim McQuade and Franklin Qian, published in the *American Economic Review* in 2019, found that covered tenants were much more likely to stay in their homes. It also found that landlords of affected buildings reduced the supply of rental housing by **15%**, by converting units to condos or redeveloping, which pushed up rents across the city ([NBER working paper w24181](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24181)). The policy helped the tenants it covered and raised costs for future renters.

**Where it stops.** Unintended consequences are an argument for careful design, not against every policy. The same study is used by people on both sides of the rent control debate, which shows that results depend on the details.

## How the whole economy works

### 13. Living standards depend on productivity

**The principle.** Over the long run, a country's standard of living depends on how much it can produce per hour of work. Higher productivity, from better tools, skills, technology and organization, lets wages rise without pushing up prices. The economist Paul Krugman summed it up in 1990: "Productivity isn't everything, but in the long run it is almost everything."

**Example.** The U.S. Federal Reserve's October 2026 statement noted that "productivity growth is strong, and capital investment is robust" ([Federal Reserve, 28 October 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20261028a.htm)). Much of that investment is in AI data centers. Whether it leads to lasting productivity gains is still an open question; the IMF lists both an AI productivity boost and "a reassessment of AI expectations" among the risks in its October 2026 outlook.

**Where it stops.** Productivity growth raises *average* income. How the gains are shared between workers and owners, and across regions, depends on bargaining power, skills and policy.

### 14. Prices rise when too much money is created

**The principle.** When the amount of money and spending grows much faster than the amount of goods and services the economy produces, prices rise. Extreme cases, such as Zimbabwe in 2008 or Venezuela in the late 2010s, followed governments printing money to pay their bills.

**Example.** This is why central banks are given independence and a price-stability goal. The Federal Reserve aims for 2% inflation over the longer run, measured by the personal consumption expenditures (PCE) price index, because steady, low inflation lets households and businesses plan ([Federal Reserve FAQ](https://www.federalreserve.gov/faqs/economy_14400.htm)).

**Where it stops.** Money growth is not the only cause of inflation in the short run. The 2026 rise in U.S. inflation, to 3.7% in September, was driven mainly by energy: gasoline prices were up 34.2% over the year ([BLS CPI, September 2026](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cpi.nr0.htm)). Supply shocks, wars and harvests move prices too. Our guide to [how inflation affects your daily life](/articles/how-inflation-affects-daily-life) explains what this means for your budget.

### 15. In the short run, there are hard trade-offs

**The principle.** When a central bank raises interest rates to bring inflation down, borrowing and spending slow, which can raise unemployment for a while. This short-run trade-off is linked to the **Phillips curve**, named after A. W. Phillips's 1958 study of wages and unemployment in Britain. Milton Friedman argued in 1968 that the trade-off does not hold in the long run: once people expect higher inflation, keeping unemployment low requires ever-faster price rises.

**Example.** In September 2026, the Federal Reserve **raised** its policy rate by a quarter point, to a range of 3.75% to 4.00%, after cutting it three times in late 2025 ([Federal Reserve, open market operations](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/openmarket.htm)). It held the rate in October; two policymakers voted for another increase. The committee has to weigh inflation of 3.7% against a job market where payrolls grew by only 29,000 in September ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/empsit.nr0.htm)).

**Where it stops.** The size of the trade-off changes over time and is hard to measure. Economists still debate how much unemployment it takes to lower inflation, and the answer depends on whether people believe the central bank will succeed.

## How to use these principles

When you read an economic news story or face a money decision, run through a short checklist:

1. **What is scarce here, and what is being given up?** (principles 1 and 2)
2. **What happens with one more unit?** Ignore what is already spent. (principles 3 and 5)
3. **How will incentives change behavior, including in ways nobody planned?** (principles 4 and 12)
4. **Is this a market where prices can do their job, or is there a market failure?** (principles 9 to 11)
5. **Is this about the short run or the long run?** (principles 13 to 15)

## FAQ

### What are the basic principles of economics?

The core ideas are scarcity, opportunity cost, marginal thinking and incentives (how individuals decide); trade, prices and market failure (how people interact); and productivity, money and short-run trade-offs (how the whole economy works). Mankiw's "ten principles of economics" is the best-known short list.

### What is the most important economic principle?

Opportunity cost is the most useful in daily life, because it applies to every choice: the real cost of anything is the best alternative you give up. For the economy as a whole, productivity matters most over the long run.

### Are economic principles always true?

No. They are good first approximations that describe how people tend to behave on average. Behavioral economics shows that people often depart from them, and every principle above has known limits. Use them as a starting point for thinking, not as laws of nature.
