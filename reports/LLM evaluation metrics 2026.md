# Measure LLMs with 25 battle-tested metrics

No single number measures an LLM in 2026. What works is a stack of **25 metrics and techniques in six families**. Statistical metrics (perplexity, accuracy, exact match) still anchor pre-training and leaderboards. Reference-overlap metrics (BLEU, ROUGE, METEOR, chrF, BERTScore) survive mainly in translation and summarization, because they **correlate weakly with human judgment on open-ended output**. Execution and calibration metrics (pass@k, ECE, Brier) cover code and confidence. Claim-level RAG and factuality metrics (faithfulness, context precision and recall, HHEM hallucination rate, FActScore, SelfCheckGPT, SimpleQA) cover grounding and truth. Model-based and human judgment (LLM-as-a-judge, Bradley-Terry arena ratings, length-controlled win rates, kappa and alpha) covers open-ended quality. Agent, safety and production metrics (task success, pass^k, METR time horizons, attack success rate with over-refusal, TTFT and ITL, error bars) cover deployment. Two things changed the picture in 2026. Benchmarks saturate fast: Stanford's AI Index 2026 reportedly found that nearly half of leading labs' evaluations no longer separate top models, and OpenAI stopped reporting SWE-bench Verified in February. The tooling also shifted: OpenAI's hosted Evals platform shuts down on 30 November 2026, and Perspective API stops after 31 December 2026. Every metric below gets a definition, a formula, a worked example where the formula is verified, its limitations and when to use it. The main lesson is that **a metric name means nothing without its implementation, judge, decoding settings and error bar**.

A note on the worked examples: they are arithmetic applied to the cited formulas, not figures quoted from the sources. Model scores come with dates and are flagged when the source is secondary.

## Statistical and reference metrics still anchor leaderboards but fail on open-ended text

The oldest metrics have exact, verifiable definitions. Most practical "gotchas" come from implementation details rather than the headline formula. EleutherAI's lm-evaluation-harness still registers 19 metrics in 2026, including perplexity, bits-per-byte, acc, acc_norm, exact_match, BLEU, chrF, chrF++, TER and Brier score ([lm-evaluation-harness metrics.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/EleutherAI/lm-evaluation-harness/main/lm_eval/api/metrics.py)). Hugging Face's lighteval adds ROUGE, BERTScore, maj@k and LLM-as-judge metrics ([lighteval metric list](https://huggingface.co/docs/lighteval/metric-list)).

### 1. Perplexity, cross-entropy and bits-per-byte

Perplexity measures how surprised a language model is by real text. It is the exponential of the mean negative log-likelihood per token, i.e. exp(cross-entropy in nats). The harness computes it as `math.exp(-mean(items))` and marks it lower-is-better ([lm-evaluation-harness](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/EleutherAI/lm-evaluation-harness/main/lm_eval/api/metrics.py)). The Pile paper defines **bits per UTF-8 byte as bpb = (L_T / L_B) · ℓ / ln 2**, where ℓ is the per-token loss in nats, L_T is the token count and L_B the byte count. The authors chose it because it does not depend on the tokenizer ([Gao et al. 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2101.00027)). *Worked example:* if a model gives four tokens probabilities 0.5, 0.25, 0.5 and 0.125, the total loss is 1+2+1+3 = 7 bits. That is a cross-entropy of 1.75 bits/token (1.213 nats), and perplexity = 2^1.75 ≈ **3.36**, the inverse geometric mean of the probabilities. If those tokens span 10 bytes, bpb = 0.70. The key limitation is that **raw perplexity is comparable only between models that share a tokenizer and evaluation text**, which is why word-perplexity, byte-perplexity and bits-per-byte exist. Use it for pre-training and language-modeling evaluation. Do not use it as a proxy for instruction-following or chat quality.

### 2. Multiple-choice accuracy (acc and acc_norm)

Accuracy is the share of items answered correctly. Benchmarks like MMLU, GPQA and HLE's multiple-choice items report it. How the "chosen" answer is picked matters. The harness's `acc` selects the option with the highest summed log-probability, which **biases selection toward shorter options**. `acc_norm` divides by the continuation's byte length, which is tokenizer-agnostic, and EleutherAI uses it across its multiple-choice tasks ([EleutherAI blog](https://blog.eleuther.ai/multiple-choice-normalization/)). Accuracy also inherits the benchmark's label noise. MMLU-Redux experts estimated that **6.49% of MMLU questions contain errors**, and 57% of the Virology subset is erroneous. Corrected labels reshuffle rankings: Palmyra X v3 moves from 4th to 1st on Virology ([MMLU-Redux, arXiv 2406.04127](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04127)). Use accuracy for benchmarks with constrained answers, and always state which normalization you used.

### 3. Exact match and token-level F1

Exact match (EM) scores 1 if the normalized prediction equals a gold answer. Token F1 gives partial credit for word overlap. The official SQuAD v2.0 script lowercases text, strips punctuation and the articles a/an/the, and collapses whitespace before comparing. F1 is computed over the overlapping token bags, and each question takes the **maximum over all gold answers** ([SQuAD evaluate-v2.0.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/rajpurkar/SQuAD-explorer/master/evaluate-v2.0.py)). The formula is P = |common| / |pred|, R = |common| / |gold|, F1 = 2PR/(P+R). *Worked example:* gold "the Eiffel Tower" against prediction "Eiffel Tower in Paris" gives EM = 0, P = 2/4, R = 2/2 and **F1 ≈ 0.667**. Names collide across tools: the harness's `f1` is a corpus-level sklearn F1 for multiple-choice tasks, not SQuAD token F1 ([lm-evaluation-harness](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/EleutherAI/lm-evaluation-harness/main/lm_eval/api/metrics.py)). lighteval's math EM strips LaTeX, and its `maj_at_k` takes a majority vote over k samples ([lighteval](https://huggingface.co/docs/lighteval/metric-list)). Use EM for short answers with a single correct form, such as GSM8K, MATH or AIME final answers. Use F1 when wording varies, as in extractive QA.

### 4. BLEU

BLEU scores n-gram precision against references. It **clips** each candidate n-gram count to the maximum count found in any single reference, so the candidate "the the the the the the the" earns 2/7, not 7/7. The formula is **BLEU = BP · exp(Σ w_n log p_n)** with N = 4 and uniform weights. The brevity penalty is BP = e^(1−r/c) when the candidate corpus length c is no longer than the effective reference length r. BP is computed **over the whole corpus**, not per sentence ([Papineni et al. 2002](https://aclanthology.org/P02-1040.pdf)). *Worked example:* with c = 12 and r = 15, BP = e^(−0.25) ≈ **0.779**, which scales every precision down by about 22%. More references raise scores. In the original paper, one human translator scored 0.3468 against four references and 0.2571 against two ([Papineni et al.](https://aclanthology.org/P02-1040.pdf)). Report it through **SacreBLEU**, which scores detokenized text and prints a reproducibility signature such as `BLEU|nrefs:1|case:mixed|eff:no|tok:13a|smooth:exp|version:2.0.0` ([sacrebleu](https://github.com/mjpost/sacrebleu)). Be careful with lighteval: its `bleu` is corpus SacreBLEU, but its `bleu_4` is averaged sentence-level NLTK BLEU, a different quantity ([lighteval](https://huggingface.co/docs/lighteval/metric-list)). The WMT22 metrics task was titled "Stop Using BLEU". It found that overlap metrics "correlate poorly with human ratings" compared with neural metrics ([Freitag et al. 2022](https://aclanthology.org/2022.wmt-1.2)). The Codex paper warned that BLEU gains "may not indicate improved rates of functional correctness" for code ([Chen et al. 2021](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2107.03374)). Use BLEU only for translation, and mainly for comparability with past results.

### 5. ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2 and ROUGE-L

ROUGE-N is recall-oriented: matched n-grams divided by total reference n-grams. ROUGE-L uses the longest common subsequence, F_lcs = (1+β²)·R·P / (R + β²·P) ([Lin 2004](https://aclanthology.org/W04-1013.pdf)). In DUC, β was set very large, so only recall counted. Modern libraries usually report F1 (β = 1). `rougeLsum` refers to the summary-level union-LCS. *Worked example (β = 1):* reference "the cat sat on the mat" against candidate "the cat on the mat" gives ROUGE-1 F1 ≈ 0.909, ROUGE-2 F1 ≈ 0.667 (3 of 5 reference bigrams match, out of 4 candidate bigrams) and ROUGE-L F1 ≈ 0.909. The limitation shows up in G-Eval's SummEval table: Spearman correlation with human judgments was only **0.192 for ROUGE-1, 0.205 for ROUGE-2 and 0.165 for ROUGE-L** ([Liu et al. 2023](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.16634)). Use ROUGE as a cheap summarization baseline. Never use it as the headline quality signal.

### 6. METEOR

METEOR aligns unigrams in stages (exact, Porter stem, WordNet synonym) and weights recall heavily: **Fmean = 10PR / (R + 9P)**. It then applies a fragmentation penalty, 0.5 · (chunks / matched unigrams)³, and scores Fmean · (1 − penalty) ([Banerjee & Lavie 2005](https://aclanthology.org/W05-0909.pdf)). *Worked example (the paper's sentence pair):* "the president spoke to the audience" against "the president then spoke to the audience" has 6 matches in 2 chunks. P = 1, R ≈ 0.857, Fmean ≈ 0.870, penalty ≈ 0.0185, score ≈ **0.853**. On DARPA/TIDES 2003 data, METEOR reached system-level Pearson **0.964** with human judgments, against 0.941 for recall alone. The authors admit the parameters "have not yet been trained to be optimal" ([Banerjee & Lavie](https://aclanthology.org/W05-0909.pdf)). Only the 2005 formula is verified here; modern variants and NLTK defaults differ. Use it when recall and synonymy matter in translation-style tasks.

### 7. chrF and chrF++

chrF works on character n-grams, which makes it robust to morphology and tokenization: **chrF_β = (1+β²)·chrP·chrR / (β²·chrP + chrR)**, with precision and recall averaged over character 1- to 6-grams. Popović's original paper favored β = 3 ([Popović 2015](https://aclanthology.org/W15-3049.pdf)). **SacreBLEU's default is β = 2**, and adding word bigrams (word order 2) gives chrF++ ([sacrebleu](https://github.com/mjpost/sacrebleu)). *Worked example:* chrP = 0.6, chrR = 0.8, β = 2 gives 2.4/3.2 = **0.75**. lm-evaluation-harness added chrF++ in v0.4.13 ([releases](https://github.com/EleutherAI/lm-evaluation-harness/releases)). Like BLEU, WMT22 groups chrF with the overlap metrics that neural metrics beat ([Freitag et al. 2022](https://aclanthology.org/2022.wmt-1.2)). Use it for translation, especially into morphologically rich languages, and always state β.

### 8. BERTScore and embedding cosine similarity

BERTScore embeds tokens of the reference and the candidate with a contextual model and greedily matches each token to its most similar counterpart. **R_BERT averages the best match for each reference token, P_BERT averages the best match for each candidate token, and F_BERT = 2PR/(P+R).** Optional IDF weighting downweights common words. Baseline rescaling, R̂ = (R − b)/(1 − b), only improves readability, not ranking ([Zhang et al. 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1904.09675)). *Worked example (illustrative similarities):* a 2-token reference and 3-token candidate with cosine rows [0.9, 0.2, 0.3] and [0.1, 0.8, 0.4] give R = 0.85, P = 0.70, **F1 ≈ 0.768**. The package defaults to `roberta-large` for English. Its README calls `microsoft/deberta-xlarge-mnli` the best model, and in its example rescaling moves F1 from 0.959 to 0.759 ([bert_score](https://github.com/Tiiiger/bert_score)). Sentence-level cosine similarity, a·b/(‖a‖‖b‖), is the coarser version of the same idea: vectors (1,2,2) and (2,1,2) give ≈ 0.889. BERTScore beats n-grams: Kendall τ 0.550 vs BLEU's 0.415 at WMT18 segment level ([Zhang et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1904.09675)). On SummEval, though, it reached only **ρ = 0.225**, far below G-Eval's 0.514 ([Liu et al. 2023](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.16634)). Describe it as "better than overlap", not "close to human". Scores also shift with the model and layer, so pin both.

## Execution, calibration and retrieval metrics reward correctness over resemblance

### 9. pass@k

pass@k is the probability that at least one of k sampled programs passes the unit tests. Computing it from exactly k samples is high-variance, so Chen et al. generate **n ≥ k samples (n = 200)**, count the c correct ones and use the unbiased estimator **pass@k = E[1 − C(n−c, k) / C(n, k)]**. The paper gives a numerically stable product form, 1 − ∏_{j=n−c+1}^{n}(1 − k/j). It shows the naive 1 − (1 − p̂)^k systematically underestimates ([Chen et al. 2021](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2107.03374)). *Worked example:* with n = 10 and c = 3, pass@1 = 0.30, pass@5 = 1 − 21/252 ≈ **0.917**, while the naive formula gives 0.832. HumanEval has 164 problems averaging 7.7 tests each. **Two Codex-12B figures must be kept apart.** Table 1 reports pass@1 = 28.81% and **pass@100 = 72.31%**. Separately, the abstract states that repeated sampling solved **70.2%** of problems with 100 samples. The abstract and the table do not match, and the 70.2% is probably a separate measurement, so cite each in its own context and do not mix them ([Chen et al. 2021](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2107.03374)). The limitation is that pass@k assumes you can check every candidate, which is fine in a benchmark and unrealistic for a user who gets one answer. HumanEval itself is now saturated (see the benchmark section). Use pass@1 for user-facing quality and pass@k for search-and-verify systems. Use the unbiased estimator, never the naive one.

### 10. Calibration: ECE, MCE and Brier score

Calibration asks whether stated confidence matches observed accuracy: P(correct | confidence = p) = p. Predictions are binned by confidence (Guo et al. used **M = 15** bins). **ECE = Σ (|B_m|/n)·|acc(B_m) − conf(B_m)|**, and MCE is the worst bin's gap. Guo et al. found that "modern neural networks are no longer well-calibrated" and proposed temperature scaling as a single-parameter fix that leaves accuracy unchanged ([Guo et al. 2017](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1706.04599)). *Worked example:* four predictions at mean confidence 0.65 with 2 correct, and six at 0.95 with 5 correct, give ECE = 0.4·0.15 + 0.6·0.117 ≈ **0.13**. The Brier score is mean squared error between predicted probability and outcome. Forecasts 0.9, 0.6 and 0.2 with outcomes 1, 0 and 0 give ≈ **0.137** in binary form. The harness's multi-class form sums over classes, so it doubles that for binary tasks ([lm-evaluation-harness](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/EleutherAI/lm-evaluation-harness/main/lm_eval/api/metrics.py)). Brier is a proper scoring rule that mixes calibration with other components, so it is **not a pure calibration measure**. ECE has its own problems: it is sensitive to the number of bins (rank correlation across bin counts is 0.37, against 0.59 for adaptive binning), and over- and under-confidence can cancel inside a bin ([Nixon et al. 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1904.01685)). Calibration matters for frontier LLMs. HLE reports calibration error alongside accuracy ([HLE paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.14249)), and SimpleQA found models "consistently overstate their confidence" ([Wei et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.04368)). Use calibration metrics whenever a system abstains, escalates or shows confidence to users.

### 11. Retrieval ranking metrics: precision@k, recall@k, MRR and nDCG

Classic IR metrics need relevance labels but no LLM. Precision@k is relevant items in the top k divided by k ([Ragas docs](https://docs.ragas.io/en/stable/concepts/metrics/available_metrics/context_precision/)). Recall@k is relevant items retrieved in the top k divided by all relevant items, which matches Ragas's ID-based context recall ([Ragas docs](https://docs.ragas.io/en/stable/concepts/metrics/available_metrics/context_recall/)). **nDCG_p = DCG_p / IDCG_p**, with DCG_p = Σ rel_i / log₂(i+1) and IDCG the DCG of the ideal ordering ([Wikipedia: DCG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discounted_cumulative_gain)). *Worked example:* binary relevance [1, 0, 1] in the top 3 gives DCG = 1 + 0.5 = 1.5 and IDCG = 1 + 0.631 = 1.631, so **nDCG@3 ≈ 0.92**. lighteval ships `recall_at_k` and `mrr` ([lighteval](https://huggingface.co/docs/lighteval/metric-list)). MRR is conventionally the mean over queries of 1/rank of the first relevant result, but that textbook formula was not verified against a primary source in this research. The limitations are real: nDCG does not penalize bad documents ([1,1,1] and [1,1,1,0] score the same), handles partial judgments poorly ([Wikipedia: DCG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discounted_cumulative_gain)), and none of these metrics checks whether the generator actually used what was retrieved. Use them to tune retrievers and rerankers when labeled data exists.

### 12. Context precision and context recall (RAGAS)

RAGAS adapts the retrieval metrics to settings without hand labels by letting an LLM judge relevance. **Context Precision@K = Σ (Precision@k × v_k) / (relevant items in top K)**, where v_k is 1 if the chunk at rank k is relevant. This is effectively average precision ([Ragas docs](https://docs.ragas.io/en/stable/concepts/metrics/available_metrics/context_precision/)). *Worked example:* relevance [1, 0, 1] gives (1·1 + 0 + (2/3)·1) / 2 ≈ **0.833**. Context recall splits the *reference answer* into claims and scores the share supported by the retrieved context ([Ragas docs](https://docs.ragas.io/en/stable/concepts/metrics/available_metrics/context_recall/)). The variants differ in how relevance is judged: an LLM checking against a reference answer, an LLM checking against the generated response (ContextUtilization), Levenshtein-based string matching, or pure ID matching. That choice changes the number. In the original paper, context relevance was the hardest dimension for the LLM to judge, with pairwise accuracy against humans of only 0.70 ([Es et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.15217v2)). Use the ID-based variants when you have gold chunk IDs, and the LLM variants for quick iteration without labels.

## Claim-level checks now define factuality, but each tool draws the line differently

Most RAG and factuality metrics share one recipe: split text into claims, verify each claim against a source, report a ratio. Ragas, DeepEval and TruLens converge on the same three checks: are the retrieved chunks relevant, is the answer supported by them, and does it answer the question ([TruLens RAG triad](https://www.trulens.org/getting_started/core_concepts/rag_triad/)). The differences hide in the verification threshold.

### 13. Faithfulness and groundedness

**Faithfulness = supported claims / total claims** in the response. An LLM extracts the claims and checks whether each "can be inferred" from the retrieved context; 1 of 2 supported gives 0.5 ([Ragas docs](https://docs.ragas.io/en/stable/concepts/metrics/available_metrics/faithfulness/)). Ragas's `FaithfulnesswithHHEM` swaps the LLM verifier for Vectara's T5-based HHEM-2.1-Open classifier. DeepEval uses the same ratio but counts a claim as truthful if it **does not contradict** the context, a looser test than Ragas's inference standard. Its small-model "System One" mode is not directly comparable with its LLM mode ([DeepEval docs](https://deepeval.com/docs/metrics-faithfulness)). TruLens calls the same idea groundedness ([TruLens](https://www.trulens.org/getting_started/core_concepts/rag_triad/)). In validation on 50-page WikiEval, Ragas faithfulness agreed with humans 95% pairwise, against 72% for a GPT-score baseline ([Es et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.15217v2)). It has three limitations. Splitting text more finely or coarsely changes the score. Scores from different tools are not comparable even when the names match. And **groundedness is not truth**: an answer that faithfully repeats a wrong document scores 1.0. Use it as the primary hallucination guard for RAG, pinned to one tool, version and judge.

### 14. Answer (response) relevancy

Answer relevancy is the one RAG metric that leans on embeddings. An LLM writes N questions that the response would answer (default 3), and the score is the mean cosine similarity between those questions and the user's actual question: **score = (1/N) Σ cos(E_gi, E_o)** ([Ragas docs](https://docs.ragas.io/en/stable/concepts/metrics/available_metrics/answer_relevance/)). The original paper used text-embedding-ada-002 and showed the metric penalizes incomplete or redundant answers ([Es et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.15217v2)). By design it **does not check factuality**, and because cosine ranges over [−1, 1], scores outside 0 to 1 are possible. DeepEval adds a useful diagnostic: high relevancy combined with low faithfulness means the model is answering from its own knowledge rather than the evidence ([DeepEval docs](https://deepeval.com/docs/metrics-faithfulness)). Use it together with faithfulness, never on its own.

### 15. Summarization hallucination rate (Vectara HHEM leaderboard)

Vectara's leaderboard has each model summarize documents using only the passage's facts, at temperature 0 where supported. HHEM-2.3 then scores factual consistency, and **hallucination rate = 100 − factual consistency rate**. An "answer rate" records how often the model summarized rather than refused. The leaderboard was updated on 22 October 2026 with a private dataset of more than 7,700 articles. The top five on that board range from **1.8% (antgroup/finix_s1_32b) to 4.1% (Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct-Turbo)** ([Vectara leaderboard](https://github.com/vectara/hallucination-leaderboard)). The authors list the limitations themselves. Results depend on HHEM agreeing with humans. A purely extractive copy would score perfectly, because the benchmark measures consistency, not summary quality. It misses some forms of hallucination, and it is English-only. Those low single-digit rates **apply only to short grounded summaries**, not to hallucination in general. Use it to compare models for grounded summarization and document-QA products.

### 16. FActScore (atomic-fact precision)

FActScore splits long-form generations into atomic facts and reports the **percentage supported by a reliable knowledge source**. Its automated estimator has under 2% error against human evaluation, and at publication **ChatGPT scored 58%** on people biographies ([Min et al., EMNLP 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14251)). It is the long-form counterpart of RAG faithfulness: it checks claims against a knowledge base rather than retrieved context. The exact length-penalty term and default knowledge source were not verified in this research. Its limitation is that it measures precision only, so a model can score highly by saying less. Use it for biographies, reports and other long factual generations.

### 17. SelfCheckGPT (sample consistency)

SelfCheckGPT is zero-resource and black-box. It samples several responses to the same prompt and flags sentences that the samples do not consistently support, on the premise that **known facts recur across samples while hallucinations diverge**. It needs no external database and no token probabilities, and it works at both sentence and passage level ([Manakul et al., EMNLP 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08896)). It follows from the method's premise that a model which hallucinates the same falsehood every time will look consistent. Sampling also multiplies inference cost. Use it when there is no reference source and no logit access, for example to monitor closed-model outputs.

### 18. SimpleQA: correct, correct-given-attempted and F-score

SimpleQA tests short-form parametric knowledge with **4,326 questions, each with a single indisputable answer** (estimated label error about 3%). A prompted classifier grades each answer as correct, incorrect or not attempted. The metrics are overall correct, correct given attempted, and their harmonic mean as an F-score ([Wei et al., arXiv 2411.04368](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.04368)). *Worked example:* 40 correct, 20 incorrect and 40 not attempted out of 100 gives overall 0.40, given-attempted 0.667 and **F ≈ 0.50**. The table shows why abstention matters. Claude-3.5 Sonnet had 28.9% correct but 35.0% not attempted, for an F-score of 35.0, while GPT-4o-mini had 8.6% correct ([Wei et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.04368)). The authors warn that the **F-score rewards guessing whenever a model is at least 50% confident**. Google DeepMind's **SimpleQA Verified** (1,000 cleaned prompts) addresses noisy labels, topical bias and redundancy. Gemini 2.5 Pro set its state of the art at F1 = 55.6 ([arXiv 2509.07968](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.07968)). Use it, preferably the Verified set, to measure short-form factual recall and abstention behavior. Whether short-form factuality predicts long-form factuality is still an open question.

## Judges and human raters scale open-ended evaluation, if you correct their biases

### 19. LLM-as-a-judge and G-Eval

LLM-as-a-judge comes in three forms: **pairwise comparison, single-answer grading and reference-guided grading** ([Zheng et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685)). G-Eval adds three refinements. The judge writes its own evaluation steps from the task criteria (auto-CoT), fills in a scores-only form, and reports a **probability-weighted score = Σ p(sᵢ)·sᵢ** ([Liu et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.16634v3)). *Worked example:* if the judge gives probabilities 0.2, 0.5 and 0.3 to scores 3, 4 and 5, the score is **4.1** rather than a tied integer. G-Eval-4 reached Spearman 0.514 on SummEval, against 0.474 for the best earlier method (UniEval). The often-quoted "over 80% agreement" with humans is the **no-tie setting**. On MT-Bench, GPT-4 agreed with experts 85% without ties and 66% with ties, against human-human figures of 81% and 63% ([Zheng et al., Table 5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685)).

The biases are well documented. Under position swapping, GPT-4 was consistent only **65.0%** of the time, and Claude-v1 23.8%. A padded "repetitive list" attack fooled Claude-v1 and GPT-3.5 91.3% of the time, and GPT-4 8.7%. On math questions it could solve itself, GPT-4 still misjudged 14 of 20 graded answers with the default prompt, against 3 of 20 with reference-guided grading ([Zheng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685)). Models can recognize their own outputs, and self-recognition correlates with self-preference ([Panickssery et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076)). Across 13 judges, Thakur et al. found that only the largest approached human alignment, all were lenient, and percent agreement alone is "insufficient" ([Thakur et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12624)). Each bias has a documented mitigation. Run both orders and declare a win only if they agree. Give a reference or CoT for reasoning tasks. Use explicit rubrics. Use a **Panel of LLM evaluators (PoLL) from disjoint model families**, which beat a single large judge across six datasets at "over seven times less" cost ([Verga et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18796)). Most hard bias numbers come from 2023–2024 judges, and no verified 2026 study re-measures them on frontier judges. Re-validate whenever the judge model or prompt changes.

### 20. Preference ratings: Elo, Bradley-Terry, win rate and length-controlled win rate

Arena leaderboards started with Elo, **E_A = 1/(1 + 10^((R_B − R_A)/400))** and R'_A = R_A + K(S_A − E_A) ([Wikipedia: Elo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elo_rating_system)). *Worked example:* a 1600-rated model facing a 1500-rated one has E ≈ 0.64, so with K = 32 a win adds about 11.5 points. Because online Elo depends on the order of battles, Chatbot Arena switched in December 2023 to **maximum-likelihood Bradley-Terry** over the full battle history, P(m beats m′) = sigmoid(β_m − β_m′), with bootstrapped confidence intervals ([LMSYS, Dec 2023](https://lmsys.org/blog/2023-12-07-leaderboard/)). In August 2024 it added **style control**, covariates for length, headers, bold and lists. Length dominated (coefficient 0.249), and rankings moved: grok-2-mini fell from 6th to 18th ([LMSYS style control](https://lmsys.org/blog/2024-08-28-style-control/)). AlpacaEval 2.0 uses the same idea. Raw win rate against a GPT-4 Turbo baseline is fit with a GLM that includes a tanh length term, and the **LC win rate** sets that term to zero. This cut the verbosity-prompt swing from 22.9–64.3% to 41.9–51.6% and raised correlation with Chatbot Arena from 0.94 to 0.98 ([Dubois et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.04475)).

Human-vote leaderboards have their own validity dispute. "The Leaderboard Illusion" alleges undisclosed private testing (Meta tested 27 variants before Llama-4) and unequal sampling ([Singh et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.20879)). Arena replied that its policy was public, that the simulated boost is about +11 points in practice, and that the 112% figure came from the static Arena-Hard benchmark, not human votes. It promised "provisional" labels until 2,000 fresh votes ([Arena response](https://arena.ai/blog/our-response)). Use preference ratings to rank chat models on open-ended quality. Read them as style-sensitive and contested, and check the confidence intervals before treating close ranks as real.

### 21. Inter-rater agreement: Cohen's kappa and Krippendorff's alpha

Agreement statistics validate both human labels and LLM judges, because raw percent agreement inflates alignment. **Cohen's κ = (p_o − p_e)/(1 − p_e)**, where p_e is the agreement expected by chance from each rater's marginals. Fleiss' kappa extends it to more than two raters ([Wikipedia: Cohen's kappa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohen%27s_kappa)). *Worked example:* two raters label 100 items, agreeing on 40 yes and 30 no, with rater A saying yes 50 times and rater B 60 times. Then p_o = 0.70, p_e = 0.50 and **κ = 0.40**: "fair", despite 70% raw agreement. **Krippendorff's α = 1 − D_o/D_e** handles any number of coders, missing data and ordinal or interval scales. α ≥ 0.800 is the usual reliability bar, and data below 0.667 are typically discarded ([Wikipedia: Krippendorff's alpha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krippendorff%27s_alpha)). The Landis–Koch bands are opinion-based, and kappa shifts with prevalence. Human agreement can itself be very low: on one G-Eval subset it was α = 0.07 ([Liu et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.16634v3)). Vendor guides quote κ > 0.6 against human labels as acceptable and > 0.8 as strong, with 100–300-sample human audits. These are heuristics, not empirical standards ([koji.so](https://www.koji.so/docs/llm-as-a-judge-vs-human-evaluation)). Report judge–human κ or α next to human–human agreement whenever an LLM judge is used.

## Agents, safety and production need reliability, trade-off and uncertainty metrics

### 22. Agent task success, pass^k and the 50% time horizon

Agent benchmarks score binary task success with execution checkers. tau-bench checks whether the final database state matches the goal. Terminal-Bench 2.0 is all-or-nothing: a task passes only if every test passes ([Inspect Evals](https://ukgovernmentbeis.github.io/inspect_evals/evals/coding/terminal_bench_2)). Reliability needs more than a mean, so tau-bench introduced **pass^k, the probability that the agent succeeds on all k independent trials** of the same task. It is the mirror image of pass@k ([tau2-bench paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07982)). *Illustration, assuming independent trials:* an agent with a 50% per-trial success rate on a task has pass^4 = 0.5⁴ ≈ **6%**. That is why the tau2-bench telecom results drop steeply as k rises ([tau2-bench repo](https://github.com/sierra-research/tau2-bench)). METR's time horizon converts success rates into **the human-expert task length at which a model succeeds 50% of the time**, by fitting a logistic curve against log human time. In Time Horizon 1.1 (January 2026), Claude Opus 4.5 reached 320 minutes and GPT-5 214. The whole-period doubling time is about **196 days**, shortening to 88.6 days since 2024 ([METR TH1.1](https://metr.org/blog/2026-1-29-time-horizon-1-1)). The caveats are large. Opus 4.5's 95% CI spans 170–729 minutes. Only 5 of 31 tasks over 8 hours have measured human baselines. GPT-4o and o3 scored higher under Vivaria than under Inspect ([METR](https://metr.org/blog/2026-1-29-time-horizon-1-1)). Scaffolds swing results further: SWE-bench Pro vendor runs report 70–80%, against about 23% under Scale's standard harness at launch ([systems-analysis.ru, secondary](https://systems-analysis.ru/eng/SWE-bench_Pro)). Report success rate together with pass^k, the harness, the attempt count and who ran the evaluation. Treat the time-horizon trend as more reliable than any single model's point estimate.

### 23. Safety: attack success rate, over-refusal and toxicity

**Attack success rate (ASR)** is the percentage of attack test cases that elicit a harmful behavior. HarmBench (510 behaviors) standardizes on greedy decoding and 512-token outputs, because generation length alone shifted substring-based ASR by up to 30%. Its fine-tuned Llama-2-13B judge agrees with humans 93.19% of the time, against 88.37% for GPT-4 ([HarmBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.04249)). StrongREJECT finds that earlier evaluations **overstated jailbreak effectiveness** and that successful jailbreaks tend to degrade the model's capabilities ([StrongREJECT, arXiv 2402.10260](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10260)). ASR has to be paired with **over-refusal**, the refusal rate on benign prompts that look harmful. XSTest's 250 prompts are now too easy, so OR-Bench supplies 80,000 seemingly toxic prompts plus a hard set of about 1,000. It found safety and over-refusal correlated at **Spearman 0.89**: the safest models also refused the most benign requests ([OR-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.20947v2)). For bias, BBQ contrasts ambiguous and disambiguated contexts. Models were up to 3.4 points more accurate when the correct answer matched a stereotype, and over 5 points for gender in most models ([Parrish et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.08193)). For toxicity, **Perspective API stops working after 31 December 2026** ([Perspective API](https://perspectiveapi.com/)). The open-source Detoxify (v0.5.4, October 2026) is maintained ([PyPI detoxify](https://pypi.org/project/detoxify/)). Teams switching should re-baseline rather than compare new numbers against old Perspective scores. Always report ASR with the judge, decoding settings, generation length and attack budget.

### 24. Latency and throughput: TTFT, ITL, TPS and RPS

Production metrics have exact formulas, but tools define them differently. In NVIDIA's definitions, **TTFT** is the time from request to first non-empty token. **End-to-end latency = TTFT + generation time.** **ITL (TPOT) = (e2e − TTFT)/(output tokens − 1).** **Per-user TPS = output tokens / e2e.** System TPS and RPS divide totals by the window from the first request to the last response, excluding warmup ([NVIDIA NIM metrics](https://docs.nvidia.com/nim/benchmarking/llm/latest/metrics.html)). *Worked example:* TTFT 0.5 s, e2e 5.5 s and 501 output tokens give ITL = 5.0/500 = **10 ms** and per-user TPS ≈ 91. Compare numbers across tools only when the definitions match. No primary source on p95/p99 reporting conventions or cost-per-task methodology was retrieved for this research. Use these metrics for capacity planning and SLAs, and track them alongside quality, because quality wins that triple latency can be net losses.

### 25. Error bars and prompt-format spread

The last technique applies to every number above. Evan Miller's "Adding Error Bars to Evals" treats questions as draws from a super-population. It recommends reporting the **standard error of the mean with the question count**, clustering standard errors when questions share a passage, comparing models with **paired question-level differences**, and sizing evals with power analysis ([Miller 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00640)). *Worked example (standard binomial arithmetic):* 80% accuracy on 500 questions has SE = √(0.8·0.2/500) ≈ 1.8 points, a 95% interval of about **±3.5 points**. Many leaderboard gaps fall inside it. Prompt format is a second source of hidden variance. Meaning-preserving format changes moved LLaMA-2-13B's few-shot accuracy by **up to 76 points**, and the sensitivity persisted with scale and instruction tuning. FormatSpread reports a performance interval across plausible formats ([Sclar et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.11324)). Harness versions matter too. lm-evaluation-harness v0.4.13 fixed group standard errors that were up to about 3× too narrow and a few-shot leak that "may shift previously reported numbers" ([releases](https://github.com/EleutherAI/lm-evaluation-harness/releases)). Use this technique everywhere. A score without an interval and a pinned configuration is an anecdote.

## Benchmarks now saturate in one to two years, and the tooling map shifted in 2026

The status of the benchmarks these metrics run on changed sharply. Coverage of the **Stanford AI Index 2026** reports that nearly half of leading companies' evaluations show saturation, defined as "loss of reliable discriminative power among top-performing models". It also reports GPQA scores around 93%, above the 81.2% expert baseline, and that hiding test data did not protect against saturation ([CloudTweaks summary, secondary](https://cloudtweaks.com/2026/05/standford-2026-ai-index/)). MMLU, GSM8K, HumanEval, MATH and vanilla needle-in-a-haystack no longer separate frontier models. RULER replaced simple needle retrieval with multi-hop and aggregation tasks ([RULER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.06654)), and SimpleQA Verified's authors describe TriviaQA, Natural Questions and TruthfulQA as saturated ([arXiv 2509.07968](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.07968)). On **23 February 2026 OpenAI stopped reporting SWE-bench Verified**. An audit of a frequently failed subset found that at least 59.4% of problems had tests rejecting correct solutions, and contamination from public repositories was also a concern. OpenAI recommends SWE-bench Pro ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/openai-wants-to-retire-the-ai-coding-benchmark-that-everyone-has-been-competing-on/)). SWE-bench Pro has 1,865 tasks from copyleft and private codebases ([Harbor hub](https://hub.harborframework.com/datasets/scale-ai/swe-bench-pro)), though a secondary report cites an unverified audit claiming about a third of its trials were mis-graded ([aiweekly](https://aiweekly.co/alerts/openai-drops-swe-bench-verified-backs-pro-after-flaw-audit)). Agents passed OSWorld's 72.4% human baseline in 2026, with figures self-reported or from aggregators that disagree on the leader ([buildmvpfast, secondary](https://www.buildmvpfast.com/blog/osworld-benchmark-ai-agents-passed-human-baseline-2026)).

The replacements are harder, refreshed or interactive. **Humanity's Last Exam** (2,500 public questions, published in Nature in 2026) now has a rolling version. A FutureHouse investigation found roughly 30% of its chemistry and biology answers questionable, which prompted HLE-Verified ([Wikipedia: HLE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanity%27s_Last_Exam); [HLE-Verified](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13964)). Artificial Analysis figures relayed by Wikipedia put top text-only HLE scores at 61.4% (Claude Opus 5.5) as of 23 October 2026. That figure is not verified at the primary source ([Wikipedia: HLE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanity%27s_Last_Exam)). **ARC-AGI-3** launched on 25 March 2026 as an interactive game benchmark with no stated rules. Humans scored 100% and frontier AI 0.51% at launch, and ARC Prize 2026 results are due on 4 December 2026 ([ARC Prize](https://arcprize.org/blog/arc-agi-3-launch)). Terminal-Bench 2.0's 89 containerized tasks left frontier agents below 65% ([arXiv 2601.11868](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.11868)). LiveBench replaces about a sixth of its questions each month ([arXiv 2605.17273](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.17273)), and LiveCodeBench scores only problems published after a model's training cutoff ([LiveCodeBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07974)).

## Conclusion

The 25 metrics form three layers. Cheap deterministic metrics (1–11) are reproducible but narrow. Claim-level and judge-based metrics (12–21) cover open-ended quality and factuality, but they import a judge's biases. Reliability and trade-off metrics (22–25) decide whether a system can be deployed. The 2026 shift is that the middle and top layers now carry most of the weight. Their numbers depend on configuration (judge model, verification threshold, scaffold, decoding length, prompt format), so **identical metric names from different tools are not the same measurement**. The most useful habit to recommend is a reporting contract: tool and version, judge, settings, attempt count, standard error and evaluation date.

Two consequences follow for anyone building an evaluation practice now. First, benchmark scores date quickly. With half-lives of one to two years and labs abandoning benchmarks they once championed, a team's own versioned golden set, scored with paired comparisons, outlasts any public leaderboard. Second, single numbers can be gamed in ways that paired numbers resist: safety alone can be gamed by refusing more, faithfulness alone by copying the context, and win rate alone by writing longer answers. Report each with its counterweight (ASR with over-refusal, faithfulness with relevancy, win rate with length control). These pairings come from the documented failure modes above, not from a published standard, but they are the most defensible way to measure an LLM in 2026.
